The data presented here describes a macroeconomic environment that appears paradoxical on the surface: disinflation is occurring, yet consumer sentiment has collapsed while spending remains resilient. To understand this, we must move beyond the headline narrative of "soft landing" and examine the structural divergence between the financial conditions that affect borrowers and the income conditions that affect savers.

### Competing Explanations and Distinguishing Factors

There are three primary competing explanations for this specific combination of high rates, falling inflation, weak sentiment, and sticky spending.

First, the **Liquidity Trap of the Wealth Effect**. This hypothesis suggests that the decline in sentiment is driven by the erosion of household net worth due to falling asset prices (equities and housing), while spending is propped up by the "buffer stock" of savings accumulated during the pandemic. The distinguishing factor here is the composition of household balance sheets. If this is the driver, we would expect to see a strong correlation between the drop in the Michigan Sentiment Index and the decline in equity market valuations or home price appreciation, rather than a direct correlation with the federal funds rate.

Second, the **Credit Channel Dislocation**. This view argues that elevated policy rates have successfully tightened financial conditions for marginal borrowers (mortgage holders, auto lenders), suppressing new demand, but have failed to impact the broader consumer base because the majority of households are not currently refinancing or taking on new debt. The spending holds up because it is funded by existing cash flow, not new credit. The distinguishing evidence would be a sharp divergence between *new* credit applications (which should be down) and *total* consumer outlays (which remain stable).

Third, the **Inflation Expectations Uncertainty**. The sharp drop in sentiment from 74 (Dec 2024) to 52.2 (Apr 2025) despite falling CPI suggests that consumers are not reacting to *current* inflation, but to *expected* future inflation or income instability. If this is the case, the sentiment drop is a leading indicator of a future spending slowdown, not a reflection of current purchasing power.

### Channels of Transmission: Who is Affected?

The data reveals a bifurcation in the transmission of monetary policy.

**The Borrower vs. The Savers:**
The effective federal funds rate has fallen from 5.33% to 4.33%, yet the 30-year mortgage rate has remained stubbornly high, hovering between 6.18% and 6.96%. This indicates that the "lock-in effect" is powerful. Homeowners with low-rate mortgages are not refinancing, meaning the high policy rate is not translating into higher monthly payments for the median existing homeowner. However, for *new* homebuyers, the cost of entry has risen significantly. This creates a two-tier housing market: existing owners are insulated, while new entrants are priced out.

**The Income-Expense Squeeze:**
Look at the divergence between Average Hourly Earnings (AHE) and Core CPI. In May 2025, AHE is up 3.98% year-over-year, while Core CPI is up 2.77%. Real wages are growing. Yet, sentiment is at multi-year lows. This suggests that the "pain" is not coming from a general loss of purchasing power across the board, but from specific sectors. The personal saving rate has remained relatively stable (5.5–6.2%), indicating that households are not depleting their buffers to maintain spending. They are saving *despite* low confidence. This is a defensive posture, not a desperate one.

### What the Conventional Reading Misses

The conventional narrative often assumes that high interest rates directly cause a recession by choking off demand. This data suggests that mechanism is broken or delayed.

1.  **Sentiment is not a reliable real-time gauge of spending power.** The Michigan Index dropped 22 points in three months (Jan–Apr 2025) while real consumer spending only slowed slightly (from 3.31% to 3.07% YoY). This decoupling implies that sentiment is reacting to *uncertainty* about the future (perhaps related to fiscal policy, trade, or labor market fragility) rather than current affordability.
2.  **Inflation is falling, but not uniformly.** Core CPI has fallen from 3.23% to 2.77%, but the 10-year Treasury yield has *risen* from 4.25% to 4.42% over the same period. This suggests that the market is pricing in a "higher for longer" policy stance or a term premium increase, which keeps long-term borrowing costs high even as short-term rates fall. The economy is being squeezed by the *term structure* of interest rates, not just the level of the fed funds rate.
3.  **The "Resilient" Consumer is a subset.** The data does not tell us *who* is spending. If the spending is concentrated in the top 20% of income earners, who have large financial assets and are less sensitive to mortgage rates, the median household may be experiencing a much sharper contraction in real disposable income than the aggregate data suggests.

### Testable Hypotheses and Required Data

To move from description to diagnosis, we need to test the following:

**Hypothesis 1: The Spending Resilience is Driven by High-Net-Worth Households.**
*   *Test:* Compare the change in real disposable income for the top 10% vs. the bottom 50% of the income distribution.
*   *Data Needed:* Quarterly Distributional National Accounts (QDINA) from the BEA, specifically the "Real Disposable Income" series by income quintile. If the bottom 50%’s real income is falling while the top 10%’s is rising, the aggregate spending data is masking a severe contraction for the majority.

**Hypothesis 2: The Sentiment Drop is Driven by Labor Market Uncertainty, Not Inflation.**
*   *Test:* Correlate the Michigan Sentiment Index with the "Job Availability" sub-index and the "Unemployment Expectations" sub-index, rather than the "Inflation Expectations" sub-index.
*   *Data Needed:* The detailed components of the University of Michigan Survey of Consumers. If the "Job Availability" component has dropped more sharply than "Inflation Expectations," the story is one of labor market fragility, not price levels.

**Hypothesis 3: The Mortgage Lock-in is Preventing Policy Transmission.**
*   *Test:* Measure the "Refinancing Rate" (the percentage of existing mortgages with rates below the current market rate) and correlate it with the change in the 30-year mortgage rate.
*   *Data Needed:* Freddie Mac’s "Refinancing Rate" series. If the refinancing rate is near 100%, it confirms that the high policy rate is not affecting the cash flow of the median homeowner, isolating the pain to new borrowers and the broader financial sector.

**Conclusion**

The data does not show a simple story of "high rates crushing the economy." It shows a complex dislocation where financial conditions are tight for new credit, but income conditions are supportive for existing savers. The collapse in sentiment is the most alarming signal, suggesting that households are bracing for a shock that the current CPI data does not yet reflect. The next story is not about whether inflation is down; it is about whether the defensive saving behavior of a fearful consumer base will eventually turn into a spending collapse, or if the real wage growth will prove sufficient to sustain demand despite the high cost of capital.
