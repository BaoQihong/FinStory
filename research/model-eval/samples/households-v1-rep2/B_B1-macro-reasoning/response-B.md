# The Confidence Gap: What the Data Can and Cannot Tell Us

## The puzzle, stated carefully

The headline pattern is real but the causal story is not yet established. Inflation has fallen — headline CPI from 2.94% to 2.68% year-on-year between July 2024 and June 2025, with a trough near 2.33% in April — while the funds rate has come down only from 5.33% to 4.33%. Real consumer spending growth has decelerated (3.03% to 2.50%) but remains positive. Sentiment, by contrast, fell from 66.4 to 52.2 between January and April 2025 before partially recovering to 60.7. Note what the table does *not* show: it does not show a recession, a spending collapse, or a credit event. It shows a mood collapse alongside a slowdown.

## Competing explanations

**1. The level-versus-rate confusion.** Households experience prices, not inflation rates. Cumulative CPI since 2021 is roughly 20% higher; a 2.7% inflation rate still means prices rising on top of that level. Sentiment may be tracking the price *level* and the difficulty of re-anchoring budgets, not the inflation *rate*. Distinguishing test: survey microdata on inflation perceptions versus expectations (University of Michigan and NY Fed surveys both ask directly), and whether sentiment correlates with the price level of frequently purchased goods.

**2. The mortgage lock-in channel.** The 30-year rate averaged 6.82% in June 2025 versus roughly 3% in 2021. Existing homeowners with low-rate mortgages are deterred from moving; would-be buyers face payment shocks. This suppresses mobility, transaction volumes, and the "trading up" that normally accompanies confidence. Distinguishing test: purchase-versus-refinance application volumes (MBA), existing-home sales and inventory, and the share of outstanding mortgages below 4%.

**3. The partisan-sentiment explanation.** Michigan's index has shown large partisan swings around elections in both directions historically. A sharp drop concentrated in one political group would be a measurement artifact of mood, not economics. Distinguishing test: the Michigan survey's partisan breakdown, published monthly.

**4. The labor-market-fragility story.** Unemployment is low (4.0–4.3%) but the *rate of change* matters: it rose from 4.0% in January to 4.3% in May. Hiring and quits rates may be falling even as the unemployment rate stays low — the "low-hire, low-fire" regime. Distinguishing test: JOLTS hires, quits, and openings; the Indeed Hiring Lab postings index.

**5. The composition-of-spending story.** Aggregate real spending growth can mask a split: affluent households (asset owners, fixed-rate mortgage holders) spending normally, while lower-income households — more exposed to credit-card rates above 20%, auto-loan rates, and rent — retrench. Distinguishing test: spending by income quintile (Bank of America and JPMorgan Chase Institute card data), delinquency rates on credit cards and auto loans (NY Fed Household Debt and Credit Report).

These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the honest position is that the table alone cannot adjudicate among them.

## How rates reach households — and where they don't

The transmission is uneven by design. **Mortgage credit**: the stock of fixed-rate mortgages is largely insulated; the flow of new borrowers is not. **Consumer credit**: credit-card and auto rates are floating or short-repriced and have stayed high even as the funds rate fell — the spread between card rates and the funds rate widened. **Business investment**: higher hurdle rates suppress marginal projects, which shows up in equipment investment and construction. **The wealth channel**: equities and home prices have been strong, supporting spending among owners — which may explain why aggregate spending held up while sentiment fell. **The dollar and global channel**: elevated US rates support the dollar, tightening conditions abroad.

The key inference: the *average* household is not the *marginal* household. The marginal household is a renter or recent buyer with floating-rate debt and no cushion — and that household is a small share of GDP but a large share of the sentiment survey.

## Who is affected differently

Renters and first-time buyers face the full force of high rates. Existing owners with low-rate mortgages are beneficiaries of lock-in (their wealth rose) and victims of it (they cannot move). Retirees with cash earn real yields not seen in years. Small businesses borrowing on lines of credit face costs that have not fallen much. The saving rate — 5.5–6.2% — is above its pre-pandemic norm, consistent with precautionary behavior concentrated somewhere in the distribution.

## What the conventional reading misses

Three things. First, **the sentiment index is a mood indicator with known partisan and media-sensitivity distortions**; treating it as a hard economic variable overstates its information content. Second, **the inflation decline is partly base effects and goods disinflation**; core CPI at 2.91% in June is not yet at target, and services inflation is stickier. Third, **the funds rate is not the only rate that matters** — the 10-year yield rose from 3.72% in September 2024 to 4.63% in January 2025 even as the Fed cut, meaning market rates partly offset policy easing. The conventional "rates are restrictive, therefore demand should weaken" story ignores that long rates did some of the tightening and then some of the loosening.

## What to test next

1. **Hypothesis: sentiment decline is concentrated by party.** Test with Michigan's partisan breakdown and the Conference Board's companion series.
2. **Hypothesis: spending divergence by income.** Test with card-spend microdata by ZIP income, plus delinquency rates by loan type.
3. **Hypothesis: lock-in suppresses mobility and construction.** Test with MBA purchase applications, existing-home sales, and residential construction permits.
4. **Hypothesis: labor demand is cooling beneath a stable unemployment rate.** Test with JOLTS hires/quits, initial claims, and the hiring-plans components of NFIB and Michigan surveys.
5. **Hypothesis: credit stress is rising at the margin.** Test with NY Fed delinquency transitions, subprime auto ABS performance, and bank charge-off rates.

The story I would commission is not "confidence collapse predicts recession." It is: **which households are actually tightening, and is the aggregate resilience a composition effect?** That is answerable with the data above — but not with this table alone.
