**The Paradox of the Purse: Why Spending Holds Up While Confidence Collapses**

The data presents a striking economic disconnect. As we move through the first half of 2025, the "hard" data on the economy remains resilient. Real consumer spending is positive, hovering between 2.4% and 3.7% year-over-year. The labor market is stable, with unemployment fluctuating narrowly between 4.0% and 4.3%. Crucially, wage growth is robust, clocking in above 4% in the spring of 2025—comfortably outpacing the headline CPI inflation rate of roughly 2.5%.

Yet, the "soft" data tells a story of profound distress. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index, which recovered slightly in late 2024 to 74, entered a freefall in early 2025, bottoming out at 52.2 in April. This level of pessimism is typically associated with recessions, yet the economy continues to expand. As we commission a feature on this divergence, we must move beyond the surface-level "soft landing" narrative to understand why consumers feel so bad while acting so relatively well.

**Competing Explanations: The "Wealth Effect" vs. The "Price-Level Trauma"**

Two primary competing explanations exist for this combination of strong spending and weak confidence.

The first is the **"Wealth Effect" shock**. While the data provided does not show asset prices, the sharp rise in the 10-year Treasury yield (from 3.72% in September 2024 to 4.63% in January 2025) suggests significant repricing in fixed income markets. If this rise in yields was accompanied by equity market volatility or a cooling housing market, consumers may feel poorer despite their rising wages. They might be spending out of necessity or habit ("revenge spending" continuing) while feeling anxious about their net worth.

The second explanation is **"Price-Level Trauma"**. Even though inflation (the rate of change) has fallen to 2.3–2.9%, the price level (the cumulative cost of goods) remains permanently higher. Consumers may not be celebrating that prices are rising slower; they may be angry that prices are not falling back to pre-pandemic levels. If real wages are rising, but not fast enough to repair the damage done by the 2021–2023 inflation spike, confidence will remain low.

To distinguish between these, we must look at the drivers of spending. If the wealth effect is dominant, we should see spending contract most among high-income households who hold the most assets. If price-level trauma is the cause, spending should be constrained specifically in discretionary categories while remaining sticky in non-discretionary goods, regardless of income bracket.

**Channels of Transmission: The Housing Lock-In**

The data reveals a critical failure in the conventional transmission mechanism of monetary policy. The Federal Reserve has lowered the effective federal funds rate from 5.33% to 4.33%, yet the 30-year fixed mortgage rate has remained stubbornly high, actually rising from 6.18% in September 2024 to 6.96% in January 2025.

This divergence suggests that the central bank's policy rate is losing its grip on long-term borrowing costs. The channel through which rates usually reach households—housing wealth—is clogged. Existing homeowners are "locked in" to low-rate mortgages taken out years ago, insulating them from the Fed's recent hikes. However, prospective buyers are facing mortgage rates near 7%.

This creates a bifurcated economy. The "locked-in" majority feels secure in their housing payments but may be pessimistic about the broader economy (driving down sentiment). The marginal buyer is shut out entirely. The transmission mechanism is working through asset price suppression (housing affordability) rather than through the cash flow of existing mortgage holders.

**Distributional Impacts: The Squeezed Middle**

The divergence between wage growth and sentiment suggests a distributional mismatch. Average hourly earnings are growing at roughly 4%, which is healthy in nominal terms. However, the personal saving rate has drifted downward, touching a low of 5.1% in December 2024 before settling around 5.7%.

This implies that consumers are maintaining their spending not by feeling wealthier, but by drawing down their savings buffer or reducing their savings rate. The "average" worker might be seeing wage gains, but if those gains are concentrated at the top of the income distribution, the "median" worker—whose sentiment drives the Michigan index—could be facing a different reality. The median household may be running on fumes, keeping spending alive via credit (not shown in the table) or reduced savings, while their confidence evaporates.

**What the Conventional Reading Misses**

The conventional reading of this data is that the economy is undergoing a "soft landing": inflation is down, jobs are plentiful, and spending is up. This view treats consumer sentiment as a lagging, noisy indicator that will eventually catch up to the "good news" of the hard data.

This reading misses the leading nature of sentiment. Historically, sharp drops in consumer confidence precede pullbacks in hiring and spending. The conventional view also fails to account for the specific pain of the "term premium." The rise in the 10-year yield (from 3.87% to 4.45% between August 2024 and February 2025) indicates that markets expect tighter financial conditions for longer, or that fiscal concerns are rising. This long-term rate pressure directly impacts business investment and housing costs in ways the Fed Funds rate does not. The market is signaling a tightening that the Fed's headline rate suggests has stopped.

**Testable Hypotheses and Required Data**

To investigate this story further, we need to test specific hypotheses regarding the sustainability of spending and the drivers of pessimism.

*Hypothesis 1: Spending is being sustained by upper-income households, masking a deterioration among lower-income cohorts.*
To test this, we need **distributional Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) data by income quintile** from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. If the bottom 60% of earners are seeing negative real spending growth while the top 20% are positive, the aggregate spending figure is misleading.

*Hypothesis 2: The rise in long-term yields (10-year Treasury) is causing a credit crunch that has not yet shown up in the unemployment data.*
We need the **Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (SLOOS)** on Bank Lending Practices. Specifically, we need to look at the net percentage of banks tightening standards for consumer loans and commercial real estate. If banks are tightening just as sentiment crashes, a credit-driven slowdown is imminent.

*Hypothesis 3: The drop in sentiment is driven by a specific shock to inflation expectations rather than current prices.*
We need the **University of Michigan 1-year and 5-year inflation expectations** components. If consumers expect inflation to spike back up despite current disinflation, their pessimism is rational (fear of the future) rather than psychological (complaint about the past).

By layering these datasets onto the foundation provided, we can determine if the economy is truly resilient, or if we are simply enjoying the final quarters of a lagging cycle before the consumer finally breaks.
