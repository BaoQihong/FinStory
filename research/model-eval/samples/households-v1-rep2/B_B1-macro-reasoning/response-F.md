**The story is uneven transmission, not a contradiction**

The evidence shows a economy in which inflation has cooled, the policy rate has come down but remains high, unemployment is stable, real consumer spending is still growing but more slowly, and consumer sentiment has fallen sharply. That combination does not fit the simple story that “high rates are crushing the consumer” or the opposite story that “the consumer is fine.” It fits a transmission story: high rates are reaching some households and sectors strongly, and others weakly or not at all. The feature should be about who is feeling the rates, through which channels, and why aggregate spending has not yet cracked.

**Competing explanations, and how to distinguish them**

First, policy may be restrictive but slow-acting, with transmission blocked by fixed-rate debt. Existing homeowners with low fixed mortgages are insulated; new buyers, renters, credit-card borrowers and small firms with floating debt are not. If this is the main story, interest-sensitive activity should be weak—home sales, mortgage applications, auto credit, small-business borrowing—while spending by older, asset-owning, fixed-rate households holds up. Delinquencies should rise among credit-dependent households, not uniformly.

Second, policy may not be very restrictive in real terms. The effective funds rate is still above the 12-month CPI change, but the gap is modest, and the 10-year yield is below the funds rate. If the neutral rate has risen, today’s policy may be only mildly restrictive. That would show up in still-positive credit growth, firm business investment, sticky core services inflation and little labor-market slack. The distinction matters: the first story says rates are biting unevenly; the second says they are not biting much at all.

Third, this may be a two-speed consumer. Aggregate real spending can hold up because high-income and asset-owning households are spending, while lower-income, renter and credit-constrained households are stressed. The falling saving rate is consistent with some households drawing down buffers, but it is also consistent with wealth effects among others. The test is distributional: spending, saving and delinquency by income, age, housing tenure and credit score.

Fourth, the sentiment collapse may be about the price level and politics, not current spending capacity. Sentiment is an index of attitudes, not a spending series. It can fall sharply when consumers are angry about cumulative prices even if their incomes and spending are adequate. It can also be partisan. The test is microdata: sentiment by party, income and expectations, compared with actual spending and price levels.

Fifth, the labor-income story may be doing the work. Unemployment is stable and wage growth is near 3.9%, so nominal incomes are still supporting spending. Disinflation may reflect supply normalization and slower price increases rather than a collapsing demand. If so, spending should track real disposable income and payrolls more closely than sentiment or rates.

**Channels**

Elevated rates reach households mainly through new borrowing, not the stock of existing debt. The 30-year fixed mortgage rate near 6.8% is a payment shock for prospective buyers and a lock-in incentive for existing owners. It reduces mobility and housing turnover, which can suppress both housing demand and supply. Consumer credit is the sharper channel: credit-card and auto-loan rates are variable or short-term, so stressed households feel policy quickly. Saving and liquidity are another channel: a lower saving rate can cushion spending for a while, but it is not a permanent buffer. Wealth is a counter-channel: asset owners may feel richer, though the table does not show wealth. Labor demand is the offsetting channel: low unemployment supports incomes and spending.

**Who is affected differently**

Existing homeowners with low fixed-rate mortgages are relatively insulated. Renters and first-time buyers face high housing costs and high mortgage payments. Lower-income and younger households are more likely to be credit-constrained and to hold variable-rate debt. Savers earn more on deposits; debtors pay more. Small businesses with floating-rate credit are more exposed than large firms with fixed debt. Regional housing markets with high prices and low supply may transmit rates differently from low-cost areas. Aggregate data hide all of this.

**What the conventional reading misses**

It misses that the relevant mortgage rate for most homeowners is not the new 30-year rate. It misses that sentiment is not spending. It misses that falling inflation does not by itself prove policy is very restrictive; the real rate may be modest. It misses that a stable unemployment rate can coexist with stress among renters, credit-card borrowers and new entrants. And it misses that “the consumer” is an average that can conceal a split economy.

**What to test next**

Testable hypotheses: (1) Mortgage lock-in is muting transmission. Test the share of outstanding mortgages below current market rates, refinancing, home sales, purchase applications and payment-to-income ratios for new buyers. Datasets: FHFA National Mortgage Database, MBA Weekly Applications Survey, Freddie Mac PMMS, Census new and existing home sales. (2) Stress is concentrated among credit-dependent households. Test credit-card and auto delinquency by age, income and credit score, and spending by income. Datasets: New York Fed Household Debt and Credit Report, JPMorgan Chase Institute spending data, Fed SHED, Consumer Expenditure Survey. (3) Sentiment is a price-level and political phenomenon. Test Michigan microdata by party and income, and compare sentiment with price levels and spending expectations. (4) Income is carrying spending. Test real disposable income, personal interest payments, payroll growth, JOLTS and wage growth by industry. Datasets: BEA Personal Income and Outlays, BLS payrolls and JOLTS. (5) Inflation stickiness is in services and shelter. Test CPI and PCE components, shelter, core services and inflation expectations. (6) Policy is only mildly restrictive. Test credit growth, business investment, bank lending standards and financial conditions. Datasets: Fed SLOOS, commercial bank credit, BEA investment data.

The feature should not ask whether high rates are hurting. They are. It should ask where, through what, and for whom—and why the aggregate has not yet shown it.
