C1. The claim does not hold as stated. Mortgage rates did fall from June to September 2024, but they rose again by January 2025 even as the fed funds rate kept falling. The evidence supports a strong link between mortgage rates and the 10-year yield in monthly changes (r = 0.83), but only a weak link with the fed funds rate (r = 0.16). The high level correlation (r = 0.93) is not evidence that cuts caused mortgage rates to fall; common trends can drive it. Confounders include inflation expectations, term premium, fiscal news and market risk appetite. To settle causality, use a longer change-based sample, control for the 10-year yield and inflation expectations, and examine event windows around Fed decisions.

C2. Not supported. The r = 0.94 figure is a correlation of levels, which can be spurious when both series trend over time. The correlation of monthly percentage changes is essentially zero (r = -0.01), so the data do not show that stocks hedge inflation month to month. Confounders include shared trends, policy regimes and the short, volatile sample. To assess hedging, compare real stock returns with unexpected inflation over longer horizons, test different lags, and use methods that address non-stationarity.

To test the claim, use a longer history with multiple inversions, pre-specified recession dates and lead–lag analysis of spreads versus recessions.
