C1. The claim does not hold as stated. Mortgage rates did fall from 6.92% in June 2024 to 6.18% in September, but much of that move preceded the September cut, and mortgage rates then rose to 6.96% by January 2025 while the fed funds rate kept falling. The change correlations support this: mortgage changes are strongly associated with 10-year yield changes (r=0.83) and only weakly with fed funds changes (r=0.16). The level correlation (0.93) is not evidence of causation; both series can trend and mortgage rates price longer-term expectations. Confounders include inflation expectations, term premia, Fed guidance, and mortgage spreads. Further analysis: event studies around FOMC dates, regressions of mortgage changes on fed funds surprises and 10-year changes, controls for spreads and lags. Verdict not supported.

C2. The claim is not supported. A 0.94 correlation of monthly levels mostly reflects common upward trends in the S&P 500 and CPI over the sample; it does not show that stocks hedge inflation. The relevant test is co-movement of returns with inflation, and monthly percentage changes have r=-0.01, essentially no relationship. A hedge would require stocks to preserve purchasing power when inflation rises, not merely to rise over the same decades as prices. Confounders include trend, monetary policy, growth, and the fact that both series are nonstationary in levels. Further analysis: real returns versus inflation surprises, horizon-specific regressions, controls for growth and rates, and tests using inflation expectations. Verdict not supported.
