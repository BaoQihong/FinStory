**C1.** Not supported as stated. The mortgage rate did fall from 6.92% (June 2024) to 6.18% (September 2024), but most of that decline happened *before* the September cut, and the rate then rose to 6.96% by January 2025 even as the fed funds rate kept falling. The change correlations confirm this: mortgage changes track 10-year yield changes (r = 0.83) far more than fed funds changes (r = 0.16). The high level correlation (0.93) is a shared-trend artifact, not evidence of pass-through. Confounding: the 10-year yield reflects growth and inflation expectations, not just policy. Further analysis: event-study regressions of mortgage changes on policy surprises, controlling for the 10-year yield.

**C2.** Not supported. The 0.94 is a correlation of *levels*, which mostly captures both series trending upward over the decade; it says nothing about whether stocks hedge inflation. On the relevant measure — monthly percentage changes — the correlation is −0.01, i.e. essentially no relationship. Confounding: common trend and non-stationarity inflate the level correlation. Further analysis: regress real stock returns on inflation surprises, or test cointegration; the near-zero change correlation already undercuts the "proven hedge" claim.

**C3.** Not supported. The sample contains a single inversion episode, so "perfect record" is a claim about n = 1 — no basis for a rule. The data also show unemployment rising only modestly (3.4% to 4.2% by August 2024, 4.5% in late 2025) while payrolls kept growing throughout (153.1m to 159.3m). That is not a clear recession. Confounding: the 2020 recession was not curve-driven, and the sample period is short. Further analysis: a longer history with multiple inversions and a formal recession-dating rule, plus controls for other leading indicators.

**C4.** Not supported as a "fixed rule." The sign flips: r = 0.07 (essentially none) in 2017–19 versus r = −0.5 in 2022–24. A relationship that changes sign across regimes is not fixed. Confounding: the 2022–24 period was dominated by inflation shocks and Fed tightening, where yields and stocks moved on the same news; 2017–19 was a low-inflation, low-volatility regime. Further analysis: a longer sample with regime indicators, or decomposing yield changes into real-rate versus inflation-expectation components.

**C5.** Insufficient evidence. With n = 6, r = −0.16 is statistically meaningless — six points cannot establish a relationship, and −0.16 is close to zero anyway. "Opposite directions" over six months is noise, not a finding. Confounding: small-sample volatility, seasonal effects, and the short window. Further analysis: a multi-year sample of monthly changes, ideally with confidence intervals and controls for income and employment.

**C6.** Not supported as stated. The 0.78 is driven by the March–July 2020 collapse, when both payrolls and spending fell together for pandemic reasons. Excluding those months, r falls to 0.27 — a weak relationship. Confounding: the COVID shock is a common cause of both series. Further analysis: re-estimate on the full sample with pandemic dummies, or use a longer post-2021 window, and check whether payroll changes lead spending changes.
