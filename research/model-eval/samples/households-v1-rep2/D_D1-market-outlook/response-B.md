# Outlook: A Late-Cycle Economy Under Pressure

The table tells a story of a policy regime that has stopped easing and started to bite. The effective federal funds rate fell from 4.09% in October 2025 to 3.63% by mid-2026, then turned back up to 3.88% in October 2026. Over the same twelve months, the 2-year yield rose from 3.52% to 4.74% and the 10-year from 4.06% to 5.13%. The 30-year mortgage rate climbed from 6.25% to 7.33%. Unemployment drifted down to 4.1%, payrolls rose from 158,408 thousand to 159,293 thousand, and real consumer spending growth held near 2.5%. But headline CPI accelerated from 2.70% in November 2025 to 4.17% in May 2026 before easing to 3.72% in September, while core CPI stayed in a 2.45–2.82% band. Consumer sentiment fell from 56.4 in January to 48.0 in October, and the saving rate dropped from 5.6% to 4.1%. The S&P 500 rose from 6,840 to 7,686 by August, then slipped to 7,580. The shape is clear: growth has held up, inflation has not fully cooperated, and the long end has repriced. What follows are conditional scenarios, not forecasts.

## Base case: sticky inflation, a higher-for-longer long end

**Conditions.** Headline CPI settles in the low-to-mid 3% range, core stays near 2.5%, and real spending growth holds around 2–2.5%. The Fed keeps the funds rate near its October 2026 level rather than resuming cuts. The 2-year and 10-year yields stay elevated, with the curve retaining its recent steepening.

**Transmission.** A policy rate that stops falling keeps short-term funding costs high. The long end stays pressured by inflation persistence and term premium. Mortgage rates remain above 7%, which suppresses turnover and refinancing rather than collapsing activity outright. Equity valuations face a higher discount rate, but earnings hold up because nominal spending is intact. The dollar stays supported by the rate differential. Cash savers earn a real return for the first time in years.

**Asset implications.** Treasuries: front-end yields anchored, long-end vulnerable to further steepening; duration offers little cushion. Equities: range-bound with pressure on long-duration growth names; the index's August-to-October pullback from 7,686 to 7,580 is the template. Housing: transaction volumes weak, prices flat to slightly down, new construction subdued. Mortgage borrowers: refinancing largely closed; existing fixed-rate holders stay locked in. Dollar: firm. Cash savers: the clearest winners, with money-market yields near the funds rate.

**What would invalidate it.** A core CPI print below 2.3% or headline below 2.5% for two consecutive months, which would reopen the easing path; or a payroll decline of more than roughly 100 thousand in a single month, which would force cuts regardless of inflation.

## Upside scenario: disinflation resumes and the Fed eases again

**Conditions.** Headline CPI falls back toward 2.5% and core toward 2.3%, consistent with the November 2025 reading of 2.70% and 2.60%. Real spending growth stays near 2.5–2.8%, as in August 2026. Sentiment recovers from its October low of 48.0.

**Transmission.** Falling inflation allows the Fed to resume cuts, pulling the 2-year down and dragging the 10-year with it. Lower mortgage rates unlock pent-up housing demand. Lower discount rates lift equity multiples, particularly in rate-sensitive sectors. A softer dollar follows the narrowing rate differential.

**Asset implications.** Treasuries: a bull steepening as the front end rallies hardest; the long end gains less. Equities: broad rally led by rate-sensitive and small-cap names. Housing: the most direct beneficiary, with transaction volumes and mortgage applications rising. Mortgage borrowers: refinancing becomes viable for recent buyers. Dollar: weaker. Cash savers: yields fall, eroding the real return advantage.

**What would invalidate it.** A reacceleration in headline CPI above 4%, as in May 2026, or a rebound in the 2-year yield above its October 2026 level of 4.74%, which would signal the market rejecting the easing narrative.

## Downside scenario: inflation forces a harder tightening

**Conditions.** Headline CPI pushes back above 4% and core above 2.8%, while real spending growth slows toward 1.5% and the saving rate stays near 4%. Sentiment remains depressed near 48. The Fed raises the funds rate further, and the long end sells off.

**Transmission.** Higher policy rates raise real borrowing costs across the curve. Mortgage rates move toward and beyond the October 2026 level of 7.33%, freezing housing. Equity multiples compress as the discount rate rises and earnings expectations soften. The dollar strengthens on rate differentials, tightening global financial conditions.

**Asset implications.** Treasuries: bear flattening initially, with the front end hit hardest; the long end may eventually rally if growth fears dominate. Equities: drawdown concentrated in cyclicals and rate-sensitive sectors. Housing: volumes fall sharply, prices soften, construction weakens. Mortgage borrowers: affordability deteriorates further; adjustable-rate borrowers face resets. Dollar: strongest in this scenario. Cash savers: nominal yields rise, but real returns depend on whether inflation outruns them.

**What would invalidate it.** A core CPI print below 2.4% alongside stable payroll growth, or a sharp rise in unemployment above 4.5%, which would make further tightening politically and economically untenable.

## The uncertainties that matter most

Three dominate. First, the inflation path: the gap between headline (3.72%) and core (2.57%) in September 2026 shows energy and food volatility masking an underlying trend that has not broken. Second, the labor market: unemployment at 4.1% with payrolls still rising is a picture of resilience, but the missing October 2025 unemployment reading and the missing October 2026 CPI and core CPI readings leave gaps in the recent record. Third, the consumer: sentiment at 48.0 and a saving rate down to 4.1% from 5.6% suggest households are spending from a thinner cushion, which makes the spending growth of 2.5% more fragile than it looks. The long end's move from 4.06% to 5.13% over twelve months is the market's verdict on all three, and it is the variable most likely to determine which scenario unfolds.
