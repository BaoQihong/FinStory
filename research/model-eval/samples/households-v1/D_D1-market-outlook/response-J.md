**Outlook: Three Paths for the US Economy**

The data through October 2026 shows an economy in a delicate balance. The effective federal funds rate has been held steady at 3.63–3.64% since March, after a gradual easing from 4.09% in October 2025. Yet long-term rates have moved in the opposite direction: the 10-year Treasury yield has climbed from 4.06% to 5.13% over the same period, and the 30-year mortgage rate has risen from 6.25% to 7.33%. Inflation, as measured by CPI, has accelerated from 2.39% in January to 3.72% by September, while core CPI has remained more subdued, ranging between 2.45% and 2.82%. Unemployment has drifted down from 4.5% to 4.1%, nonfarm payrolls have grown modestly, and consumer sentiment has been volatile, dipping to 44.8 in May before recovering to 55.2 in July.

These readings point to three plausible paths forward.

**Base case: gradual normalization.** The Fed maintains its current policy stance, allowing the economy to cool at the margin without a sharp contraction. Inflation drifts back toward 2.5% over the course of a year as the effects of earlier easing fade and labor market conditions ease. The transmission mechanism is straightforward: stable policy rates anchor expectations, the yield curve remains moderately inverted or flattens, and the dollar holds its ground. For Treasuries, this implies yields that rise modestly as the curve normalizes but do not spike. US equities, supported by steady earnings and a resilient consumer, drift higher in a range-bound fashion. Housing and mortgage borrowers face continued pressure as the 30-year rate stays above 7%, limiting affordability and slowing transaction volumes. The dollar remains broadly stable, and cash savers see real returns that are positive but modest, given the gap between the funds rate and inflation.

This scenario is invalidated if CPI or core CPI breaks decisively above 3.5% for two consecutive months, or if unemployment rises above 4.5% while payrolls decline for three straight months.

**Upside scenario: reacceleration.** The economy proves more resilient than expected. Payrolls accelerate, unemployment falls below 4%, and consumer spending grows at a pace above 3% year-over-year. The transmission mechanism is one of demand pulling prices higher: stronger income growth feeds into wage pressures (average hourly earnings already rose 3.12% in October), which in turn push up goods and services prices. The Fed, faced with a second wave of inflation, pauses or reverses its easing bias. Treasuries sell off, with the 10-year yield moving above 5.5% as the market prices in a higher-for-longer policy path. Equities, particularly growth and technology names, come under pressure as discount rates rise, though cyclicals may outperform. Housing is hit hardest: mortgage rates could push above 8%, freezing the market and weighing on homebuilder earnings. The dollar strengthens on higher US rates, and cash savers benefit from higher nominal yields, though the real return depends on how far inflation climbs.

This scenario is invalidated if the personal saving rate falls below 3.5% while real consumer spending growth stalls below 1.5%, signaling that household balance sheets are constraining demand.

**Downside scenario: demand shock.** A sudden deterioration in confidence or a financial stress event triggers a sharp contraction. Consumer sentiment, already volatile, breaks below 45 and stays there. The transmission mechanism is a negative wealth and income spiral: falling equity prices reduce household balance sheets, spending contracts, firms cut hiring, and unemployment rises. The Fed responds by cutting rates aggressively, but the initial shock is felt in credit markets before policy can fully offset it. Treasuries rally as a flight to safety pushes the 10-year yield below 4%, while the 2-year yield falls even more sharply, steepening the curve. Equities fall sharply, with small caps and financials hit hardest. Housing enters a correction as mortgage rates fall but demand collapses; home prices soften. The dollar weakens as global risk aversion rises and US growth prospects dim. Cash savers see nominal yields fall, but if inflation also declines, the real return may remain positive.

This scenario is invalidated if nonfarm payrolls show three consecutive months of gains above 100,000 while unemployment stays at or below 4.2%, indicating that the labor market is absorbing the shock.

**Key uncertainties.** The most important unknowns are the path of inflation, particularly whether core CPI, which has been more stable, will follow headline CPI higher; the resilience of the labor market, given that unemployment is near multi-year lows; and the behavior of long-term rates, which have moved independently of the Fed's policy stance. The divergence between the 2-year and 10-year yields, and the continued rise in mortgage rates despite a stable funds rate, suggests that market expectations of future inflation and growth are shifting. Whether this reflects a genuine reacceleration or a temporary supply-side shock will determine which of these scenarios materializes.
