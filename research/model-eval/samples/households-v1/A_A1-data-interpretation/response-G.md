### Part 1 — Interpretation

This dataset tracks US macroeconomic indicators from September 2024 through October 2026, providing a window into the evolving pressures on US households. The period is characterized by a persistent inflationary environment, a shifting interest rate regime, and a notable deterioration in consumer sentiment despite nominally improving labor market conditions.

Price pressures remained a constant burden. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) rose every month except for June 2026, when it ticked down from 333.979 to 332.568. Core CPI (excluding food and energy) followed a similar upward trajectory, indicating that inflation was broad-based and not merely driven by volatile commodity prices. By late 2026, the headline CPI had risen from 314.732 to 337.476, a cumulative increase of approximately 7.2%.

The labor market showed nominal strength but mixed real outcomes. Nonfarm payrolls grew steadily from 157.9 million to 159.3 million, and the unemployment rate remained relatively stable, fluctuating between 4.0% and 4.5%. Average hourly earnings rose consistently from $35.34 to $38.00. However, the household benefit of these raises was eroded by inflation. While earnings grew, the cost of living rose concurrently, and the personal saving rate declined from 5.7% in September 2024 to 4.1% in October 2026, suggesting households were spending a larger share of their income to maintain consumption.

A significant divergence appears between economic activity and consumer psychology. Real personal consumption expenditures (inflation-adjusted) rose steadily from $16,165.2 billion to $17,003 billion. Yet, the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index collapsed over the period, falling from 70.1 in September 2024 to 48.0 in October 2026. This suggests that while households continued spending, their outlook on their financial well-being and the economy darkened considerably.

The cost of borrowing fluctuated. The effective federal funds rate trended downward for much of the period, falling from 5.13% in September 2024 to a low of 3.63% before ticking back up to 3.88% in October 2026. Mortgage rates were more volatile, ending higher (6.24% in November 2025 to 7.33% in October 2026) than they began (6.18% in September 2024), increasing the debt service burden on new homebuyers.

Limitations of this data include the absence of household debt levels, income distribution (median vs. average), and housing affordability metrics beyond mortgage rates. The missing CPI and unemployment data for October 2025 creates a gap in calculating continuous inflation and labor trends.
