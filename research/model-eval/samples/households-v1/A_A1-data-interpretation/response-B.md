### Part 1 — Interpretation

Over the twenty-eight months shown, US households experienced a distinct shift from a high-rate, high-confidence environment to one of easing monetary policy, persistent inflation, and deteriorating sentiment. The data reveals a divergence between the labor market, which remained remarkably stable, and the financial conditions and psychological outlook of consumers, which weakened significantly.

The most striking trend is the collapse in consumer confidence. The University of Michigan sentiment index began at 70.1 in September 2024, dipped to 57.0 in March 2025, and then entered a sustained decline, hitting a low of 44.8 in May 2026 before ending at 48.0 in October 2026. This represents a drop of over 22 index points from the start of the period. This decline occurred even as the unemployment rate remained low, hovering between 4.0% and 4.5%, and nonfarm payrolls grew steadily from 157.9 million to 159.3 million. This divergence suggests that households were not reacting to job losses, but rather to the cost of living and financial conditions.

Inflation remained a persistent headwind. Headline CPI rose from 314.7 in September 2024 to 337.5 in October 2026, a cumulative increase of approximately 7.2%. While the annual rate of inflation moderated from roughly 3.0% in late 2025 to 2.4% by late 2026, it never approached the typical 2% target. Core CPI followed a similar path, indicating that underlying price pressures were sticky. Meanwhile, average hourly earnings grew from $35.34 to $38.00, a nominal increase of about 7.5%. Because nominal wage growth slightly outpaced headline inflation over the full period, real wages likely saw modest gains, but the lag in price adjustments and the high level of absolute prices would have squeezed household budgets, particularly for those on fixed incomes.

Financial conditions eased significantly over the period. The effective federal funds rate fell from 5.13% in September 2024 to 3.88% in October 2026, a decrease of 125 basis points. However, the 30-year fixed mortgage rate did not fall in tandem; it actually rose from 6.18% to 7.33% over the same period. This divergence is critical for household well-being, as it implies that the cost of housing remained prohibitively high, likely suppressing home purchases and increasing the burden on variable-rate debt. The personal saving rate also declined from 5.7% to 4.1%, suggesting that households were consuming a larger share of their disposable income, possibly to maintain consumption levels in the face of higher costs, or that they were drawing down buffers.

Limitations of this data are significant. It does not capture income distribution; the average hourly earnings figure masks potential polarization between high and low-wage workers. It also lacks data on household debt levels, asset prices (such as equities or housing values), and regional variations. Furthermore, the sentiment index is a survey-based measure that can be influenced by news cycles and does not directly measure financial health. Finally, the data is aggregate; it does not show how these trends affected different demographic groups, such as renters versus homeowners, or those with fixed versus variable-rate debt.
