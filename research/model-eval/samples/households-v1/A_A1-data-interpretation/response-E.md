Part 1 — Interpretation

Over the 26 months from September 2024 to October 2026, the data describe a household sector that is, on balance, still expanding but under mounting pressure from prices, with sentiment and the saving rate telling a more troubled story than the labour market.

Inflation. Headline CPI rose from 314.732 to 337.476, a 12-month rate that fell from about 2.9% in September 2025 to about 1.9% by September 2026 — disinflation, but not deflation. Core CPI (ex food and energy) moved from 320.732 to 339.9, and its 12-month rate stayed roughly a percentage point above headline throughout, meaning underlying price pressure remained the dominant component. The CPI index never fell in any month of the sample; it rose in 25 of 26 months, with the only exception being a flat-to-negative move in June 2026 (332.568 vs 333.979 in May 2026).

Income. Average hourly earnings rose from $35.34 to $38.00, a 12-month growth rate of about 5.3% in September 2026. But because CPI rose about 1.9% over the same window, real earnings growth was roughly 3.3% — positive, but well below the nominal rate. Earnings growth was steady, never negative, and never below 4% in any 12-month window shown.

Labour market. Nonfarm payrolls rose from 157,912 thousand to 159,293 thousand, about 1.4 million jobs over 26 months, but the pace slowed markedly: the monthly change was +133 thousand in October 2024, +165 thousand in November 2024, and only +165 thousand in October 2026 after a dip to −170 thousand in October 2025. The unemployment rate drifted from 4.1% to 4.1%, peaking at 4.5% in November 2025 — a mild deterioration, not a recession.

Sentiment and saving. The University of Michigan index fell from 70.1 in September 2024 to 48.0 in October 2026, a 22-point decline, with the lowest reading of 44.8 in May 2026. The personal saving rate fell from 5.7% to 4.1% over the same span, hitting 3.6% in September 2026. These two series are the clearest signal of household stress: even as nominal incomes rose and unemployment stayed low, consumers became markedly less confident and saved less.

Divergences. The gap between nominal earnings growth (~5%) and real earnings growth (~3%) widened as inflation stayed above 1.5%. The gap between the federal funds rate (cut from 5.13% to 3.88%) and the 30-year mortgage rate (which rose from 6.18% to 7.33% over the same period) widened substantially, meaning housing affordability deteriorated even as the central bank eased.

Limitations. This table has no data on household debt, housing prices, wealth, income distribution, or the composition of consumption. A falling saving rate could reflect either distress or a deliberate shift to spending; sentiment is a survey, not a behavioural measure. The data do not show whether the households gaining from wage growth are the same households losing from mortgage-rate increases.
