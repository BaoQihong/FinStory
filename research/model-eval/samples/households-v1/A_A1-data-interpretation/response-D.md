**Part 1 — Interpretation**

The data from September 2024 through October 2026 depicts a period of mounting financial pressure on US households, characterized by accelerating inflation, a softening labor market, and rising borrowing costs.

While the economy continued to expand—Real Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) rose from approximately $16.2 trillion to over $17.0 trillion—the underlying trends for individual consumers deteriorated. Inflation, as measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI), did not subside. Instead, it re-accelerated. The 12-month CPI inflation rate rose from roughly 3.0% in September 2025 to 3.7% by September 2026. This increase was driven largely by volatile items, as core inflation (excluding food and energy) lagged behind headline inflation by 1.2 percentage points in late 2026.

Households found their wage growth insufficient to offset these rising prices. Although nominal average hourly earnings grew steadily—reaching 3.2% year-over-year by September 2026—this failed to keep pace with the 3.7% surge in prices. Consequently, real earnings fell by approximately 0.5% over that same period, eroding purchasing power.

Simultaneously, the labor market lost momentum. The unemployment rate climbed from 4.1% in late 2024 to a peak of 4.5% in late 2025, remaining elevated throughout 2026. Nonfarm payroll growth was anemic, averaging only a few tens of thousands of jobs per month, indicating reduced demand for labor.

Perhaps the most striking divergence occurred in interest rates. The Federal Reserve cut the effective federal funds rate significantly, from 5.13% to a low of 3.63% by mid-2026. However, households did not benefit from these cuts; the 30-year fixed mortgage rate moved in the opposite direction, surging from 6.18% to 7.33% by October 2026. This suggests that factors other than the short-term federal funds rate—such as inflation expectations or long-term growth outlooks—were driving borrowing costs higher, making housing less affordable.

To maintain their consumption levels amidst these headwinds, households reduced their savings. The personal saving rate trended downward, dropping from 5.7% to a low of 3.6% in September 2026. This drawdown in savings, coupled with plummeting consumer sentiment (which hit a low of 44.8 in May 2026), indicates that households were increasingly financially fragile.

It is important to note the limitations of this data. These figures are national aggregates; they mask significant distributional differences, as high-income households may be insulated from trends that severely impact low-income earners. Furthermore, the data does not account for household debt levels, which are critical for assessing total financial health. Finally, while Real PCE suggests spending is up, it does not reveal whether this spending is discretionary or merely the result of higher prices for necessities.
