C1. The claim that mortgage rates "came down" because of Fed cuts is not supported by the data. While the Fed funds rate fell from 5.33% in August 2024 to 4.33% in January 2025, mortgage rates were 6.5% in August and rose to 6.96% by January. The strongest statistical relationship is between mortgage rates and the 10-year Treasury yield (r = 0.83 for changes), not the fed funds rate (r = 0.16 for changes). Mortgage rates track long-term bond yields, which are influenced by inflation expectations and term premia, not just short-term policy rates. The high correlation of levels (r = 0.93) is spurious cointegration of two trending series. To settle this, analyze the spread between mortgage rates and 10-year yields over time.

C2. The claim that stocks are a proven inflation hedge is not supported. The high correlation of levels (r = 0.94) is spurious; both CPI and stock prices trend upward over time. What matters for a hedge is whether stock returns move with inflation, and the correlation of monthly percentage changes is -0.01 — effectively zero. Stocks and inflation move independently in the short run. Confounding factors include inflation expectations already priced into equities and the impact of inflation on discount rates. To settle this, examine real (inflation-adjusted) stock returns over multiple inflation regimes.

C3. The claim that a recession followed the yield curve inversion is not supported by the provided data. While the 10-year minus 2-year spread was negative from July 2022 to August 2024, the macroeconomic indicators do not show recessionary conditions. Nonfarm payrolls grew from 153 million in July 2022 to 159 million in October 2026, and unemployment remained relatively low (4.1% in October 2026). A recession typically involves rising unemployment and contracting payrolls. The "perfect record" claim is unsupported by this sample. To settle this, apply the NBER's multi-variable recession definition to the full dataset.

C4. The claim that rising yields are "bad for stocks" as a "fixed rule" is not supported. The evidence shows the relationship is unstable: the correlation between S&P 500 returns and 10-year yield changes was positive (r = 0.07) from 2017–2019 and negative (r = -0.5) from 2022–2024. Whether rising yields hurt stocks depends on the economic regime — in growth phases, yields may rise alongside stocks; in inflation shocks, they may move inversely. To settle this, analyze the correlation conditional on inflation regimes and earnings growth.

C5. The claim that sentiment "doesn't matter" for spending is not supported. The evidence covers only six months (r = -0.16), which is an insufficient sample size to dismiss a behavioral relationship. Short-term divergences between sentiment and spending are common — consumers may maintain spending due to inertia, contractual obligations, or wealth effects despite feeling pessimistic. A six-month window cannot invalidate a structural economic relationship. To settle this, examine the correlation over a full business cycle (3–7 years).

C6. The claim that payroll changes drive spending is weakly supported but requires qualification. The full-sample correlation is 0.78, but this is heavily distorted by the pandemic: excluding March–July 2020 drops the correlation to 0.27. The apparent strong relationship was driven by the extreme co-movement of layoffs and spending collapses during COVID-19 lockdowns. The underlying relationship is modest. To settle this, perform Granger causality tests to determine if payroll changes lead spending changes.
