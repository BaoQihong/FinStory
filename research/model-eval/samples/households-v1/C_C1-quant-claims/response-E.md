C1. The claim overstates the evidence. The fed funds rate was unchanged at 5.33% from June to August 2024, then fell to 4.33% by January 2025. Mortgage rates did not steadily “come down”: they fell from 6.92% to 6.18% by September, then rose to 6.96% by January. The monthly-change correlation between mortgage rates and the 10-year yield is high (0.83), while the change correlation with the fed funds rate is low (0.16). The high level correlation (0.93) is likely a common-trend artifact. The data support that mortgage rates track long-term yields more than short-term policy rates. Confounders include term premium, inflation expectations, Treasury supply, and mortgage-market spreads. To settle it, compare mortgage rates to 10-year yields plus spread, run event studies around FOMC cuts, and test whether policy-rate changes add explanatory power after controlling for long yields.

C2. The claim is not supported. A 0.94 correlation between monthly levels of the S&P 500 and CPI is almost certainly a spurious trending-series correlation: both nominal series rise over time. The economically relevant comparison is changes or real returns, and the correlation of monthly percentage changes is essentially zero (-0.01). That does not prove stocks are not an inflation hedge, but it refutes the stated “proven” claim. Confounders include nominal price trends, business-cycle movements, and the fact that CPI and stock prices can both trend upward without co-moving month to month. Further analysis should use real S&P returns, inflation-adjusted index levels, longer samples, cointegration tests, and possibly sector or dividend-adjusted returns. The evidence supports a level correlation, not a hedge.

C3. The claim is not supported by the supplied evidence. The 10-year/2-year spread was negative for 26 months, but the sample contains only one inversion episode, and the labor data do not show a recession: unemployment rose from 3.4% to 4.5% but then fell to 4.1%, while payrolls increased from 153,072 thousand to 159,293 thousand. A single episode cannot establish a “perfect” record, and the given data do not show output contraction. Confounders include term premium, fiscal supply, monetary policy, and the fact that unemployment and payrolls are imperfect recession indicators. To settle it, use multiple inversion episodes, NBER business-cycle dates, real GDP or output gap, lag distributions, and controls for why the curve inverted. The evidence supports an inversion, not a recession or a perfect predictive record.

C4. The claim is not supported as a fixed rule. The correlation between S&P 500 returns and changes in the 10-year yield is near zero in 2017–2019 (0.07) and negative in 2022–2024 (-0.5). The sign and strength are period-dependent, and each sample is only 36 months. Rising yields may be bad when they reflect inflation, tightening, or deteriorating growth, but they may be benign or positive when they reflect stronger growth or lower term premium. Confounders include monetary policy, earnings, risk appetite, and liquidity. Further analysis should use a longer sample, control for macro variables, test for regime dependence, and distinguish yield changes from yield levels. The evidence supports a conditional relationship, not a fixed rule.

C5. The claim is not well supported. A six-month correlation of -0.16 between monthly changes in consumer sentiment and monthly percentage changes in real spending is weak and based on only six observations. It does not establish that sentiment “doesn’t matter”; it only shows a slight negative co-movement over a very short window. Confounders include income changes, prices, credit conditions, fiscal policy, and measurement error. A six-month sample cannot identify a stable relationship or rule out sentiment effects. Further analysis would need a longer sample, significance tests, controls for income and prices, lead-lag tests, and robustness to different sentiment measures. The evidence is too thin to support the broad claim.

C6. The claim is not supported as a causal statement. The 0.78 correlation between monthly payroll changes and real-spending growth is high, but it falls to 0.27 when March–July 2020 is excluded, indicating the relationship is heavily driven by the pandemic shock, when jobs and spending both collapsed and rebounded. Correlation does not establish that jobs “drive” spending; both may respond to common shocks such as income, policy, health restrictions, and confidence. Confounders include the 2020 break, fiscal transfers, and simultaneous macro shocks. Further analysis should use longer samples, controls, distributed lags, Granger causality, and possibly instruments or structural-break tests. The evidence supports a strong association, especially around 2020, not a proven causal driver.
