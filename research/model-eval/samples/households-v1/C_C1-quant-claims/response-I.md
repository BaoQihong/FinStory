**C1.** The claim is not supported. While mortgage rates fell alongside the initial Fed cut in September 2024, they subsequently rose through January 2025 even as the Fed continued cutting. The high correlation of levels ($r=0.93$) merely reflects a shared long-term upward trend prior to this period, not a causal transmission mechanism. The evidence suggests long-term market expectations, rather than short-term policy, drove mortgage rates in this instance.

**C2.** The claim is not supported. The high correlation of levels ($r=0.94$) is spurious, driven by both series rising over time due to economic expansion and inflation, rather than the stocks hedging the inflation itself. The relevant metric for "hedging"—whether stock returns move *with* inflation—shows a correlation of effectively zero ($r=-0.01$). This indicates that month-to-month fluctuations in inflation do not correspond with stock returns. Stocks have not acted as an inflation hedge in this period; they have simply trended upward alongside the general price level.

Since payrolls grew and unemployment remained relatively contained, the standard definition of a recession (significant economic decline) is absent. The yield curve's record is imperfect in this specific instance, as the signal did not precede a contraction.

The relationship is likely confounded by the *reason* yields are rising—whether due to strong growth (which helps stocks) or inflation fears (which hurts stocks). A single coefficient cannot capture this nuance.

The evidence is insufficient to dismiss sentiment's importance for spending.
