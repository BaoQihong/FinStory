**C1.** The claim is not supported. Mortgage rates did fall from 6.92% in June 2024 to 6.18% in September, but much of that move preceded the September Fed cut, and from September 2024 to January 2025 the fed funds rate fell from 5.13% to 4.33% while the 30-year mortgage rate rose from 6.18% to 6.96%. The change correlations tell the same story: mortgage changes are strongly related to 10-year yield changes (r = 0.83) but weakly related to fed funds changes (r = 0.16). The levels correlation of 0.93 is not evidence that cuts caused mortgage rates to fall; both series can trend together for other reasons. Confounders include long-term yields, inflation expectations, term premia and mortgage spreads. Further analysis would use event windows around Fed decisions and regressions of mortgage-rate changes on fed funds and 10-year changes.

**C2.** The claim is not supported. A correlation of 0.94 between the levels of the S&P 500 and the CPI mostly reflects common trends over time, not an inflation hedge. The correlation of monthly percentage changes is -0.01, which is essentially no relationship. An inflation hedge would require stock returns, especially real returns, to hold up when inflation rises or surprises upward; the evidence given does not show that. Confounders include trend, monetary policy, growth expectations and the difference between expected and unexpected inflation. Further analysis would regress stock returns or real stock returns on inflation surprises and CPI changes, at multiple horizons, with controls for growth and rates.

**C3.** The claim is not supported as stated. The evidence shows a 26-month inversion from 2022-07 to 2024-08, but it does not establish that a recession followed. Unemployment rose from 3.4% in 2023-04 to 4.2% in 2024-08 and 4.5% in 2025-11, then fell to 4.1% in 2026-10, while payrolls rose from 153,072 thousand to 159,293 thousand. That is labor-market cooling and payroll growth, not a demonstrated recession. More importantly, the sample contains only one inversion episode, so it cannot establish a “perfect” record. Further analysis would need formal recession dates, a historical sample with multiple inversions, false-positive cases and lag distributions.

**C4.** The claim is not supported. The relationship is not a fixed rule: the correlation between monthly S&P 500 returns and monthly changes in the 10-year yield was 0.07 from 2017 to 2019 and -0.5 from 2022 to 2024. The sign and strength changed across periods, so “rising bond yields are bad for stocks” is not a stable empirical rule. The 2022–24 negative correlation may reflect a particular inflation-and-Fed-tightening regime, but the earlier near-zero correlation shows the relationship is regime-dependent. Confounders include growth shocks, inflation shocks, Fed reaction functions and risk premia. Further analysis would use rolling regressions and decompose yield changes into real-rate and inflation-expectation components.

**C5.** The evidence is insufficient. A correlation of -0.16 based on six monthly observations is weak and very fragile; it cannot support the broad claim that consumer sentiment “doesn’t matter” for spending. It also does not firmly establish that sentiment and spending “moved in opposite directions” in any economically meaningful sense. Small samples are sensitive to one or two months and to timing. Confounders include income, employment, inflation, interest rates and lagged effects of sentiment on spending. Further analysis would use a longer sample, lagged correlations, controls for income and employment, and disaggregated spending categories.

**C6.** The claim is not supported as a general statement. The full-sample correlation of 0.78 is high, but excluding March–July 2020 reduces it to 0.27. That pattern indicates the headline correlation is heavily driven by the pandemic period, when payrolls collapsed and spending collapsed together because of shutdowns and policy support. Outside that episode, the relationship is much weaker. Confounders include COVID shutdowns, fiscal transfers, composition effects and outliers. Further analysis would use robust methods, exclude or model the pandemic, test lagged relationships and control for income and fiscal policy.
