**The Divergence Paradox: Why Falling Inflation Isn’t Translating to Consumer Relief**

The data presented here describes a macroeconomic environment that defies the standard textbook transmission mechanism. We are observing a classic "soft landing" scenario on the surface: inflation is cooling (CPI down from 2.94% to 2.68% YoY), unemployment remains historically low (4.1–4.3%), and real consumer spending is still growing (2.5–3.7% YoY). Yet, the psychological and financial temperature of the household sector is plummeting. Consumer sentiment has collapsed from 74 in December 2024 to 52.2 in May 2025, while the personal saving rate has stagnated or risen slightly, suggesting households are hoarding cash rather than spending it.

This divergence—falling prices but falling confidence, with spending holding up only by inertia—points to a structural disconnect between the financial system and the real economy. To understand this, we must move beyond the aggregate view and examine the competing explanations for why elevated rates are not yet suppressing demand, even as they erode household balance sheets.

### Competing Explanations: Liquidity Trap vs. Wealth Effect

The primary competing explanation for this pattern is whether the current spending resilience is driven by **wealth effects** or **liquidity constraints**.

If the economy is being sustained by the wealth effect, we would expect high-net-worth individuals to continue spending despite high rates, as their asset values (stocks, real estate) remain high. In this scenario, the deterioration in consumer sentiment is a "rich man’s problem," driven by volatility in financial assets, while the broader economy remains insulated.

Conversely, if the economy is facing a **liquidity constraint**, the resilience in spending is likely a lagged response to previous income growth, or is being propped up by credit card debt and auto loans. In this scenario, the collapse in sentiment is a leading indicator of an impending sharp drop in spending. The fact that the personal saving rate has not dropped significantly (hovering around 5.5–6.2%) suggests that households are not drawing down savings to maintain consumption; rather, they are saving more out of precaution. This points toward the liquidity constraint hypothesis: households are feeling the squeeze of high interest payments on variable-rate debt and are cutting discretionary spending *before* it shows up in the aggregate real spending data.

To distinguish between these, we must look at the composition of spending. If the wealth effect is dominant, we should see continued strength in luxury goods and services. If liquidity constraints are binding, we should see a shift toward essential goods and a rise in delinquencies on consumer credit.

### The Transmission Channel: Why Rates Aren’t Hitting Harder

The conventional view is that the Federal Reserve’s policy rate (currently 4.33%) should transmit to the broader economy through the discount rate on mortgages and consumer loans. However, the data reveals a broken transmission mechanism.

The 30-year fixed mortgage rate has remained stubbornly high (6.65–6.96%) despite the Fed cutting rates from 5.33% to 4.33%. This is because mortgage rates are not set by the Fed; they are set by the 10-year Treasury yield plus a spread. The 10-year yield has actually *risen* from 4.25% in July 2024 to 4.42% in May 2025, reflecting market expectations of fiscal deficits and persistent inflation. Consequently, the "housing channel" of monetary policy is effectively shut. Homeowners are not refinancing, and new buyers are priced out. This means the Fed’s cuts are not reaching the housing market, which is a major driver of household wealth and spending.

Furthermore, the spread between the effective federal funds rate and the 10-year Treasury yield has widened. This suggests that the market is pricing in a "term premium" that the Fed cannot control. The Fed is cutting short-term rates, but long-term rates— which determine the cost of capital for businesses and the cost of housing for consumers— are rising. This decoupling means that the "price of money" for households is not falling, even as the headline policy rate drops.

### Who Is Affected Differently?

The data suggests a bifurcation of the household sector.

1.  **Fixed-Rate Debtors:** Households with fixed-rate mortgages or student loans are largely insulated from the current rate environment. Their payments are unchanged. Their sentiment may be driven by stock market performance, which is not in the data but is implied by the wealth effect hypothesis.
2.  **Variable-Rate Debtors:** Households with credit cards, auto loans, and adjustable-rate mortgages are feeling the full brunt of the 4.33% policy rate. As these rates remain elevated, their monthly payments are rising. This group is likely driving the collapse in consumer sentiment. They are the ones cutting back on spending, but their share of total consumption is smaller than that of the wealthy, so the aggregate spending data does not yet reflect their pain.
3.  **Savers:** The personal saving rate has not fallen, suggesting that households are not borrowing to maintain consumption. This is a sign of caution, not confidence. Savers are benefiting from higher interest rates on deposits, but this benefit is likely being offset by the cost of living and the uncertainty of the future.

### What the Conventional Reading Gets Wrong

The conventional reading of this data would be: "Inflation is falling, the Fed is cutting rates, and the economy is soft-landing. The drop in consumer sentiment is a temporary blip."

This reading is wrong because it ignores the **lag structure** of consumer behavior. Consumer sentiment is a forward-looking indicator; it reflects expectations of future income and job security. The fact that sentiment has collapsed while unemployment remains low suggests that households are anticipating a future downturn, possibly due to the high cost of debt or the lack of wage growth relative to inflation.

Moreover, the conventional reading misses the **fiscal dimension**. The rise in the 10-year Treasury yield suggests that the market is demanding a higher premium for holding long-term government debt. This is a signal of fiscal stress, not just monetary policy. If the government is running large deficits, it is bidding up long-term rates, which offsets the Fed’s cuts. This "fiscal dominance" means that the Fed’s ability to stimulate the economy is being neutralized by the Treasury’s borrowing.

### Testable Hypotheses and Next Steps

To test these hypotheses, we need to move beyond the aggregate data and look at the distribution of debt and spending.

**Hypothesis 1: The spending resilience is driven by the top 10% of income earners.**
*   **Test:** Compare the real spending growth of the top decile vs. the bottom 50% of income earners. If the top decile is spending more than 3% YoY while the bottom 50% is spending less than 1% YoY, this supports the wealth effect hypothesis.
*   **Dataset:** Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) Personal Income and Outlays by income group, or CreditBureau data on spending by income cohort.

**Hypothesis 2: The collapse in consumer sentiment is driven by variable-rate debt delinquencies.**
*   **Test:** Correlate the drop in consumer sentiment with the delinquency rates on credit cards and auto loans. If delinquencies are rising in tandem with the sentiment drop, this supports the liquidity constraint hypothesis.
*   **Dataset:** Federal Reserve G.19 Consumer Credit data, specifically the delinquency rates on revolving and installment credit.

**Hypothesis 3: The 10-year Treasury yield is rising due to fiscal deficits, not inflation expectations.**
*   **Test:** Decompose the 10-year yield into its components: real yield, inflation expectations (TIPS breakeven), and term premium. If the term premium is rising, this supports the fiscal dominance hypothesis.
*   **Dataset:** Federal Reserve H.15 release, which provides the term premium estimates.

**Hypothesis 4: The personal saving rate is rising because households are paying down debt, not because they are saving for the future.**
*   **Test:** Look at the "debt service ratio" (debt payments as a percentage of disposable income). If this ratio is rising, it suggests that households are using their income to service debt, which would explain why the saving rate is not falling despite high spending.
*   **Dataset:** Federal Reserve Z.1 Financial Accounts, specifically the debt service ratio for households.

In conclusion, the data suggests that the US economy is in a state of **stalled transmission**. The Fed is cutting rates, but the benefits are not reaching the household sector due to the decoupling of short-term and long-term rates. The collapse in consumer sentiment is a warning sign that the current spending resilience is fragile and may not be sustainable. The next few months will likely see a sharper drop in consumer spending as the lagged effects of high rates fully materialize.
