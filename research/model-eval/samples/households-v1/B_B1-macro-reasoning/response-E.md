**The Great Decoupling: Why Confidence Crashes While Spending Holds**

The data presents a paradox that defies standard macroeconomic transmission models. Between mid-2024 and mid-2025, the Federal Reserve successfully lowered the effective federal funds rate by nearly 100 basis points, and headline inflation cooled to near-target levels. Yet, the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index collapsed from 74.0 to 52.2, a decline of nearly 30%. Simultaneously, real consumer spending growth slowed but remained positive, hovering between 2.4% and 3.7%. This divergence suggests that the traditional link between interest rates, inflation expectations, and consumer behavior has fractured. To commission a feature on this anomaly, we must move beyond the narrative of "lagged policy effects" and investigate the structural breaks in household balance sheets and labor market dynamics.

**Competing Explanations: The Wealth Effect vs. The Affordability Trap**

Two primary hypotheses explain why sentiment plummets while spending persists. The first is the "Affordability Trap." This view argues that while inflation rates (the change in prices) have declined, the price level (the absolute cost of goods) remains historically high. For households, the pain is not in the rate of change but in the cumulative loss of purchasing power. The data supports this: while CPI 12-month changes fell from 2.94% to 2.68%, the average hourly earnings growth (3.86% in June 2025) barely outpaces core inflation (2.91%). Real wage growth is thin. Consumers are not spending because they feel wealthy; they are spending because they have no choice, drawing down excess pandemic savings to maintain a standard of living that is increasingly out of reach.

The second hypothesis is the "Labor Market Anxiety" model. Unemployment has remained remarkably stable, fluctuating only between 4.0% and 4.3%. However, stability in the unemployment rate does not equate to job security. If hiring rates have slowed or if wage growth is decelerating in key sectors, workers may perceive their future income streams as fragile. The sharp drop in sentiment in early 2025, coinciding with a plateau in the federal funds rate at 4.33%, suggests that households are pricing in a higher risk of recession than the current data reflects. They are not reacting to current conditions, but to a perceived fragility in the labor market that the headline unemployment rate masks.

**Transmission Channels: The Mortgage Rate Disconnect**

The most striking failure of monetary policy transmission is visible in the housing market. The Fed cut rates aggressively from July 2024 to December 2024, lowering the effective federal funds rate from 5.33% to 4.48%. Yet, the 30-year fixed mortgage rate, which should track long-term expectations, barely budged. It fell to 6.18% in September 2024 but rebounded to 6.82% by June 2025.

This disconnect indicates that the transmission mechanism is blocked at the long end of the curve. The 10-year Treasury yield, a proxy for long-term expectations, also failed to fall significantly, rising from 3.72% in September 2024 to 4.38% in June 2025. This suggests that the market is not pricing in a return to low-rate equilibrium. Instead, it is pricing in persistent fiscal deficits or a term premium that has structurally risen. For households, this means the "wealth effect" from housing is negative. Homeowners are locked in low-rate mortgages, creating a "golden handcuff" effect that discourages mobility, while renters and first-time buyers are priced out entirely. This bifurcation drives sentiment down (as the majority feel excluded) while spending holds up (as the locked-in homeowners continue to consume).

**Distributional Divergence: The K-Shaped Reality**

The data reveals a stark distributional split. The personal saving rate has declined from 6.2% to 5.5%, indicating that households are dissaving to maintain consumption. This is not a sign of strength but of exhaustion. The "K-shaped" recovery is now a K-shaped contraction in sentiment. High-income households, whose assets are tied to equities and real estate, may be less affected by the sentiment crash. However, the middle and lower-income cohorts, who spend a higher marginal propensity on essentials like food and energy, are feeling the pinch of the cumulative price level.

The stability of the unemployment rate hides this distress. If we assume that job quality has deteriorated—more part-time work, fewer benefits, or slower wage growth in service sectors—the "average" worker is worse off than the headline numbers suggest. The collapse in sentiment is likely driven by those on the margin of the labor market, who are anxious about their ability to keep up with costs, even if they haven't yet lost their jobs.

**What the Conventional Reading Misses**

The conventional reading of this data would conclude that the Fed is "soft landing" the economy: inflation is down, unemployment is low, and growth is positive. This view misses the psychological and structural damage. It ignores that sentiment is a leading indicator of discretionary spending. A sentiment index of 52.2 is historically associated with recessions. The fact that spending hasn't collapsed yet is likely due to the lagged effect of pandemic savings, which are now dwindling (as seen in the falling saving rate). The conventional view also misses the political economy implications: a population that feels poorer despite "good" macro data is a population that is politically volatile and economically fragile.

**Next Steps: Testing the Hypotheses**

To validate these theories, we need data that goes beyond aggregates.

1.  **Hypothesis:** The sentiment crash is driven by the "Affordability Trap" and cumulative price levels, not current inflation.
    *   **Test:** Compare the Consumer Sentiment Index against a "Cost of Living Index" that tracks the absolute price level of essential goods (food, energy, rent) rather than the rate of change. If sentiment correlates more strongly with the absolute level of these costs than with CPI inflation, the affordability trap is confirmed.

2.  **Hypothesis:** The labor market is stable in quantity but fragile in quality, driving anxiety.
    *   **Test:** Analyze the JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey) data for the "quits rate" and "hiring rate." A declining quits rate suggests workers are afraid to leave their jobs, indicating anxiety. Additionally, look at the distribution of wage growth by percentile. If median wage growth is lagging mean wage growth, it confirms that the "average" worker is falling behind.

3.  **Hypothesis:** The mortgage rate disconnect is due to a structural rise in the term premium, not just Fed policy.
    *   **Test:** Decompose the 10-year Treasury yield into real yields and inflation expectations using TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) data. If real yields are rising while inflation expectations are stable, it confirms a structural shift in the risk-free rate, which policy cannot easily reverse.

This feature should not ask "Is the economy strong?" but rather "Why does the economy feel broken when the metrics say it is working?" The answer lies in the gap between statistical averages and household reality.
