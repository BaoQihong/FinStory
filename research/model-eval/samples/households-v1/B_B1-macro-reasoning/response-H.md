**The Great Disconnect: Resilient Spending Amidst a Confidence Crash**

The economic data from July 2024 through June 2025 presents a puzzle that defies the standard post-war business cycle logic. We are witnessing a stark divergence: "hard" data on consumption and employment remains robust, while "soft" data on consumer psychology has collapsed. Inflation is moderating, and the labor market is stable, yet the University of Michigan’s sentiment index has plummeted from a post-pandemic high of 74.0 in December 2024 to a recessionary 52.2 by April 2025. As we commission a feature on this trend, we must look beyond the aggregate numbers to understand why the economy continues to spend while its participants lose faith.

**Competing Explanations: Income vs. Uncertainty**

Two primary narratives compete to explain this divergence. The first is the "Income Shield" hypothesis. The data shows that Average Hourly Earnings are growing at roughly 4.0% year-over-year, significantly outpacing Core CPI, which has drifted down to roughly 2.8%. In real terms, households have more purchasing power than they did a year ago. Under this view, spending is driven by cash flow, and the collapse in sentiment is merely noise—a reflection of political noise or media pessimism rather than economic reality.

The second explanation is the "Term Premium Shock" hypothesis. While the Federal Reserve has lowered the Effective Federal Funds Rate from 5.33% to 4.33%, the 10-year Treasury yield has paradoxically risen from 3.72% to 4.42% over the same period. Mortgage rates have followed, climbing back above 6.8%. This suggests that while short-term policy has eased, long-term financial conditions have tightened. Here, the drop in confidence reflects a rational response to the cost of capital—specifically housing and long-term debt—even if current wages allow for day-to-day consumption.

To distinguish between these, we must determine if the consumer is spending because they feel secure (Income Shield) or because they are compelled to maintain lifestyle standards despite rising long-term costs (Term Premium Shock).

**Transmission Channels: The Housing Wedge**

The most critical channel through which elevated rates are reaching the economy is housing, but its impact is asymmetric. The 30-year fixed mortgage rate is the primary mechanism of monetary transmission for the middle class. The data shows that as the Fed cut rates in late 2024, mortgage rates briefly dipped to 6.18%, only to surge past 6.9% in early 2025.

This surge creates a "lock-in" effect that suppresses mobility and impacts confidence without immediately crushing consumption. Households feel poorer because the asset value of their home is sensitive to these rates, and the prospect of trading up becomes impossible. However, because most homeowners hold low-rate mortgages fixed years ago, their monthly cash flow is unaffected. This explains why Real Consumer Spending remains positive (hovering near 3%) while sentiment crashes: the "wealth effect" from housing has turned negative, but the "cash flow effect" from employment remains positive.

**Distributional Effects: The Skew of Resilience**

The aggregate data hides a deep distributional split. The "Personal Saving Rate" has bumped along between 5.1% and 6.2%. This is historically low, suggesting that the resilience in spending is not driven by the wealthy increasing their consumption of luxury goods, but by the broader population dipping into savings or maintaining marginal consumption to keep up with the cost of living.

We are likely seeing a bifurcated economy. The "haves"—those with homes locked in at low rates and stock portfolios—may be driving the sentiment drop due to asset price volatility, while the "have-nots"—renters and prospective buyers—are seeing their purchasing power eroded by sticky rents and high borrowing costs. However, because the labor market (Unemployment at 4.1-4.2%) remains tight, the "have-nots" are still employed and spending, preventing a downturn. The misery is concentrated in the *psychology* of future mobility (housing) rather than the *reality* of current solvency (wages).

**What the Conventional Reading Misses**

The conventional reading treats Consumer Sentiment as a leading indicator for spending. If confidence drops, a recession is presumed to follow. This data suggests that relationship has broken down. The conventional view also underestimates the lag effect of monetary policy. The Fed began cutting rates in September 2024, yet the economy reacted as if rates were rising in early 2025 due to the movement in long-term yields.

Furthermore, analysts often conflate "disinflation" with "price stability." While the 12-month CPI change is down to 2.3%, the price *level* remains high. Consumers may be reacting to the cumulative loss of purchasing power over the last three years, even if the *rate* of increase has slowed. The data does not show the price level, only the change, but the sentiment plunge suggests consumers do not distinguish between "prices rising slowly" and "prices being high."

**Testable Hypotheses and Required Data**

To verify these inferences, we need to look beyond the aggregates provided. I propose testing the following hypotheses:

1.  **Hypothesis:** The rise in spending is being funded by a depletion of excess savings or a rise in revolving credit, rather than current income.
    *   **Required Data:** We need the Federal Reserve’s G.19 release on Consumer Credit (specifically revolving credit, e.g., credit cards) and the Distributional Financial Accounts to see changes in liquid asset holdings by income quintile.

2.  **Hypothesis:** The divergence in sentiment is driven specifically by the "lock-in" effect and housing affordability, rather than general economic anxiety.
    *   **Required Data:** We need the "Buying Conditions for Houses" index component from the University of Michigan survey, alongside the National Association of Realtors' Housing Affordability Index.

3.  **Hypothesis:** The rise in the 10-year yield (and mortgage rates) is driven by fiscal concerns or term premium expansion rather than expectations of growth.
    *   **Required Data:** We need the 10-year breakeven inflation rate (to separate inflation expectations from real yields) and data on long-term Treasury auctions to measure foreign demand.

The story is not that the economy is booming, nor that it is crashing. It is that the consumer is spending in spite of themselves, propped up by a tight labor market, while the cost of shelter and capital erodes their optimism. The feature should focus on the fragility of this equilibrium: how long can spending hold up when confidence is this low?
