# The Price of Money Hasn't Gone Down

*Headline inflation is falling again, but households feel poorer because the cost of borrowing money has not reset to the pre-pandemic era.*

The financial headlines are obsessed with the rate of change, but households live with the level. It is a distinction that explains the gap between the economic data and the public mood. Prices have risen 29.8% since January 2020, a cumulative climb that has reshaped the cost of daily life. Yet, on paper, the average worker is winning. Average hourly earnings are up 33.2% over that same period. In theory, a typical worker has more money in their pocket than they did before the pandemic, even after accounting for higher grocery bills. The squeeze, then, is not entirely about what people buy. It is about what they borrow.

The cost of borrowing has not gone back to where it was. A household that took out a 30-year fixed mortgage in January 2021 locked in a rate of 2.74%. Today, that same mortgage costs 7.33%. The price of money has more than doubled. This is the structural change that the "inflation is falling" narrative misses. Even as the pace of price increases slowed, the price of credit remained elevated, locking new buyers out of the market and trapping current owners in place. When you combine this with the fact that headline inflation is now accelerating again, the financial pressure on households intensifies.

The recent reprieve was brief. Consumer prices rose 3.0% in the 12 months to September 2025, then dropped to a low of 2.4% in January 2026. It looked like a return to normalcy. But by September 2026, that rate had climbed back to 3.7%. This is not just a statistical blip; it is a reversal that hits where it hurts most. Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, sits at 2.6%. This means the recent surge is concentrated in the volatile categories people notice most often: the gas pump and the checkout aisle.

The University of Michigan tracks this divergence. "Since mid-2022, consumer sentiment has been moving more closely with sentiment regarding higher prices than with sentiment regarding income," their surveys report. People are not looking at their wage growth and celebrating; they are looking at the price of eggs and the size of their monthly payments and feeling poorer.

The wage data supports this unease. While pay has outpaced prices over the long term, the momentum has stalled. In the year to September 2026, hourly earnings grew 3.2%. That is slower than the 3.7% rise in consumer prices over the same period. The average worker is losing purchasing power again. The cushion earned over the previous years is being eroded just as the cost of living ticks up.

The Federal Reserve is caught in a dynamic that does not respond easily to interest rate cuts. The central bank held the federal funds rate at 3.63% from May through August 2026. Since then, it has raised the rate to 3.88%. This is a modest increase, yet it signals that the era of loose money is definitively over. This tightness transmits directly to the housing market. The 30-year fixed mortgage rate, which was 6.25% in October 2025, has jumped to 7.33% a year later. Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody’s Analytics, recently noted the weight of this sector: "Perhaps the largest contributor to the outsized inflation reading in Tuesday's CPI report was housing."

Housing acts as a transmission belt for monetary policy. When the Fed raises rates to fight inflation, it makes mortgages more expensive, which adds to the cost of shelter, which keeps inflation elevated. It is a feedback loop. The homeowner with a 2.74% mortgage is insulated, but the renter or the first-time buyer faces a market where financing costs have tripled. This creates a two-tier economy: those who locked in cheap credit before 2022, and those who are paying the price for it now.

The mystery is why the economy hasn't buckled under this weight. Real consumer spending — adjusted for inflation — is up 2.5% in the 12 months to October 2026. People are still buying things. The answer lies in the savings rate. The personal saving rate, the share of disposable income people put away, has fallen from 4.9% in October 2025 to 4.1% a year later. Households are dipping into their savings to maintain their standard of living.

This behavior is visible in shifting habits. Research on consumer behavior notes a progression: "Primarily, consumers started trading down, but as inflation’s impact on consumers persisted, broader behaviors started to change; these included less frequent purchases, shifting from restaurants to eating at home." The resilience in spending numbers is likely a result of this drawdown. You cannot finance your lifestyle by reducing your savings buffer indefinitely.

The psychological toll is evident in the sentiment data. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index sits at 48 in October 2026. This is a remarkably low number for an economy with a 4.1% unemployment rate. It is also a partial recovery from the depths of May 2026, when the index hit 44.8, the lowest reading in at least a decade. But compared to the start of last year, when the index stood at 71.7, the mood has collapsed. When people are pessimistic despite having jobs, the culprit is usually the cost of maintaining their lifestyle rather than the fear of losing their income.

The path forward depends on whether the uptick in inflation proves temporary or persistent. If the rise is driven by one-off shocks in energy, the psychological blow might fade. But if the 3.7% rate sticks, the pressure on the Federal Reserve will increase. Another rate hike would push mortgage costs even higher, worsening the housing squeeze.

The critical variable to watch is the personal saving rate. If it drops below 4.1%, households are running out of runway. The economy is currently being propped up by people spending the money they used to save. When that buffer is gone, the reality of higher borrowing costs and stagnant real wages will hit consumption directly. The argument that "inflation is falling" will offer little comfort then. The price level is permanently higher, and the price of money is rising with it.
