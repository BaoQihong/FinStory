# The Permanent Squeeze

*Americans have technically outpaced inflation with their wages, but the cost of borrowing has rewritten the household balance sheet.*

For the past several years, the economic narrative has rested on a single number: inflation. When inflation was high, the story was about hardship; when it fell, the story was about progress. But for the average household, the relief implied by a falling inflation rate has largely failed to arrive. People still feel squeezed, and they are right to feel that way. The disconnect is that inflation is a rate of change, but households live with the level of prices.

Between January 2020 and September 2026, the CPI rose 29.8% cumulatively. Average hourly earnings rose 33.2% over the same period. On a simple basis, pay has more than kept pace with prices. Yet the feeling of precariousness persists because the cost of borrowing — the price of accessing capital — has not returned to previous norms, and it now intersects with a second surge in consumer prices.

The optimism of early 2026 was premature. Headline inflation fell to 2.4% in the 12 months to January 2026, a low point that suggested the battle was nearly won. By September 2026, however, CPI was 3.7% for the preceding 12 months, up from 3.0% a year earlier. Core inflation, excluding food and energy, stood at 2.6% by September 2026. This means the recent acceleration is concentrated in the goods people buy most frequently, giving the price surge a visibility that core inflation masks.

At the same time, the window where wages were gaining ground has closed. Average hourly earnings grew 3.2% in the 12 months to September 2026. With headline inflation at 3.7%, the average worker’s pay is losing purchasing power again.

The real trauma of the last five years is not found in the grocery store alone, but in the cost of borrowing. A household that took out a mortgage in January 2021 faced a rate of 2.74%. By October 2026, the 30-year fixed mortgage rate was 7.33%, up from 6.25% in October 2025.

For anyone entering the housing market or attempting to move, the effective cost of homeownership has risen sharply, independent of the price of the home itself. This is not limited to housing. The Federal Reserve held its funds rate at 3.63% through August 2026 and has since raised it to 3.88%. Borrowing across the board is more expensive.

This creates a situation where people who locked in low rates years ago are insulated, while everyone else faces a new reality. The squeeze is the cumulative weight of 29.8% higher prices and borrowing costs that have more than doubled from their 2021 lows.

Consumers are absorbing this by spending their savings. Real consumer spending — adjusted for inflation — rose 2.5% in the 12 months to October 2026. This suggests demand remains resilient, but the funding source is eroding. The personal saving rate fell to 4.1% in October 2026, down from 4.9% in October 2025. Households are sustaining their standard of living by saving less, a strategy with a finite runway.

The sentiment data shows the result. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index was 48 in October 2026, down from 71.7 in January 2025. It hit a low of 44.8 in May 2026, the lowest reading since at least 2016 in our data. With unemployment at 4.1% in October 2026, the pessimism is not driven by job insecurity. It is driven by the daily experience of eroded purchasing power.

As the University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers noted, "Since mid-2022, consumer sentiment has been moving more closely with sentiment regarding higher prices than with sentiment regarding income." People care less about whether their raises are keeping pace with inflation than they do about the fact that prices are high.

The behavior of consumers is adapting, though not in a way that suggests recovery. Research on consumer behavior indicates that "Primarily, consumers started trading down, but as inflation’s impact on consumers persisted, broader behaviors started to change; these included less frequent purchases, shifting from restaurants to eating at home."

The upward turn in inflation is particularly damaging because it reinforces the belief that the cost-of-living crisis is permanent rather than transitory. Mark Zandi of Moody’s Analytics, quoted by CNBC, noted that "Perhaps the largest contributor to the outsized inflation reading in Tuesday's CPI report was housing." When housing costs drive inflation, the transmission to household hardship is more direct, as shelter is the largest expense for most families.

What happens next depends on whether the current inflation upturn persists. If the 3.7% reading continues to climb, the Federal Reserve may be forced to raise rates further, deepening the credit squeeze even as the economy cools. If inflation moderates, the relief may still be illusory — falling inflation does not lower the price level, nor does it automatically lower mortgage rates for those already locked into high-cost debt.

The saving rate is the variable to watch. At 4.1% and falling, it suggests the cushion that sustained spending throughout 2025 and 2026 is thinning. When savings are exhausted, spending will have to adjust to actual income, and the resulting deceleration could trigger the very recession that aggressive rate hikes are designed to avoid.

The central bank is operating on the assumption that higher rates will tame inflation and that the labor market can absorb the shock. But for millions of households, the math of 2026 is simple: prices are high, borrowing is expensive, and the savings are running out. The macroeconomic indicators may eventually stabilize, but the standard of living for the average American has shifted permanently downward.
