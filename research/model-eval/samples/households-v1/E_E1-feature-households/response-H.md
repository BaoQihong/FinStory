# Why Americans feel poorer even when inflation falls

*The rate was falling. The level wasn't. That distinction is the whole argument.*

The most common complaint from American households in 2026 is not that prices are rising fast. It is that prices are still high. The inflation rate — the speed at which prices rise — fell from 3.0% in the year to September 2025 to 2.4% by January 2026, a low point. Then it rose back to 3.7% by September 2026. That is a meaningful swing, and it matters, but it is not the story most households are telling.

The story is this: since January 2020, the CPI price level has risen 29.8%. Average hourly earnings over the same stretch are up 33.2%. On paper, pay has more than kept up with prices. So why does the household feel squeezed? Because the squeeze is less about the grocery bill than about the cost of getting onto, or moving up, the housing and credit ladder — and that cost has not gone back.

A household that took out a 30-year mortgage in January 2021 faced a rate of 2.74%. In October 2026, the rate was 7.33%. In October 2025 it had been 6.25%. A rate is a price: it is what the government or a bank charges to lend you money, and it compounds over the life of the loan. A household locked in at 2.74% has little reason to refinance or sell, and a household trying to buy for the first time in 2026 is pricing against 7.33%. The two groups are living in different economies, and the second one is the one that feels the pinch.

That is the argument this piece makes: the "inflation is falling" story was always a story about the rate, while households live with the level. And in 2026 the level is rising again, faster than a year ago.

The evidence, read carefully, points the same direction.

Inflation re-accelerated to 3.7% in the 12 months to September 2026, up from 3.0% a year earlier and from the 2.4% low of January. Core inflation — which excludes food and energy, the purchases people notice least often — is 2.6%. So the recent rise is concentrated in food and energy, exactly the line items that show up on every receipt. Mark Zandi of Moody's Analytics put it plainly, quoted by CNBC: "Perhaps the largest contributor to the outsized inflation reading in Tuesday's CPI report was housing." Housing is both a purchase and a cost of living, and it is showing up in the data as a drag on affordability.

Wages are not keeping up. Average hourly earnings grew 3.2% in the 12 months to September 2026 — slower than prices, so the average worker's pay is again losing purchasing power. That is a small but real loss, and it compounds on top of a price level that is already 29.8% higher than it was in 2020.

Borrowing costs tell the same story from the other side. The 30-year mortgage rate was 6.25% in October 2025 and 7.33% in October 2026. The Federal Reserve held its policy rate at 3.63% through August 2026 and has since raised it to 3.88%. Bond yields — what the government pays to borrow — are the anchor for long-term rates, and they have not fallen back to where they were in 2021. So a household that bought a home in 2021 is still paying a rate near 2.74%, while a household buying in 2026 is paying 7.33%, and the spread between them is the gap between two different markets.

Households are still spending. Real consumer spending is up 2.5% on a year earlier. But the personal saving rate fell from 4.9% in October 2025 to 4.1% in October 2026. Some of that spending is being financed by saving less. That is not a sustainable position, and it is a useful signal: households are borrowing against their own future to keep the current bill paid.

Sentiment confirms the squeeze. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index was 48 in October 2026, against 71.7 in January 2025 and a low of 44.8 in May 2026 — the lowest reading in our data since at least 2016. Unemployment is 4.1%, so this is not a jobs story. The Michigan survey has noted, in its own research, that "Since mid-2022, consumer sentiment has been moving more closely with sentiment regarding higher prices than with sentiment regarding income." That is a useful summary of what households are reacting to: the level, not the rate.

The likeliest explanation for the 2026 re-acceleration is that the rise is concentrated in food, energy and housing — the line items that show up on every receipt — rather than a broad-based return to the 2022 pattern. Core inflation at 2.6% suggests the underlying trend has not yet turned upward; the headline number is being lifted by the items people notice most. That is a reading of the data, not a certainty: we do not know whether the rise will persist.

What we do know is what households are doing about it. A research note on consumer behaviour describes the shift: "Primarily, consumers started trading down, but as inflation's impact on consumers persisted, broader behaviors started to change; these included less frequent purchases, shifting from restaurants to eating at home." That is not a panic; it is a slow adjustment. Households are not running for the exits; they are tightening the belt and staying put.

The thing to watch is the saving rate. At 4.1%, it is low, and it is falling. If it keeps falling while spending holds steady, households are borrowing against their own future to keep the current bill paid — and that cannot continue indefinitely. If it stabilises, the squeeze is being absorbed by lower spending, which is a different and more sustainable posture. The next few months of saving-rate data will tell us which way the household is leaning, and that is the number that matters most.
