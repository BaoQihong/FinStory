**C2. Stocks are a proven inflation hedge**

If stocks were a hedge, we would expect a negative correlation between inflation surprises and stock returns (stocks falling when inflation rises unexpectedly) or a positive correlation if inflation signaled growth. The near-zero correlation suggests stocks neither consistently protect against nor benefit from inflation in the short term. Confounding factors include monetary policy responses to inflation. Further analysis should use detrended data or cointegration tests to determine if a long-run equilibrium relationship exists.

**C4. Rising bond yields are bad for stocks**
The claim is not supported as a fixed rule. The correlation between S&P 500 returns and changes in the 10-year yield varies significantly by period: it was near zero (r=0.07) in 2017-2019 but strongly negative (r=-0.5) in 2022-2024. This regime dependence suggests the relationship is conditional on macroeconomic context, such as inflation expectations or monetary policy stance. In low-inflation environments, rising yields may signal growth (positive for stocks), while in high-inflation environments, they signal tightening (negative for stocks). Treating it as a "fixed rule" ignores this structural break. Confounding factors include the reason for yield changes (growth vs. inflation). Further analysis should model the relationship with interaction terms for inflation regimes or monetary policy cycles.

Confounding factors include lag effects and measurement noise. Further analysis requires a much longer time series (years, not months) to assess the stability and magnitude of the relationship between sentiment and spending.
