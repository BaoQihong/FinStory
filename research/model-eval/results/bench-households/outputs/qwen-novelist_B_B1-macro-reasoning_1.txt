The headline pattern — falling inflation, still-high policy rates, resilient spending, collapsing confidence — is internally consistent with at least three different stories, and the data above cannot yet tell us which one is true.

**Competing explanations.** The first is the benign one: the Fed has done its job, inflation is converging to target, and the confidence drop is a one-off reaction to a specific shock (a tariff announcement, a political event, a market wobble) that has not yet shown up in behaviour. Under this reading, spending should keep holding up, the labour market should stay tight, and confidence should recover within a few months. The second is the demand-destruction reading: elevated rates are finally working, but through balance-sheet and credit channels rather than through the labour market, so spending is being sustained by households running down savings or borrowing against home equity while confidence falls because people sense the squeeze coming. The third is the stagflation-adjacent reading: inflation is falling but still above target, real rates are high, and the confidence collapse is an early signal of a coming labour-market deterioration that the unemployment rate has not yet captured.

What would distinguish them: under the benign story, the saving rate should be stable or rising and mortgage applications should recover; under the demand-destruction story, the saving rate should fall and credit-card balances should rise; under the stagflation story, job openings and initial claims should deteriorate before the headline unemployment rate does.

**Channels.** The data show the policy rate falling from 5.33% to 4.33% over twelve months, yet the 30-year mortgage rate has barely moved (6.85% to 6.82%) and the 10-year Treasury has actually risen (4.25% to 4.38%). That gap is the key fact. It means the Fed's easing is not being transmitted to the two rates that matter most for household balance sheets: long-term housing credit and long-term borrowing costs more generally. The 10-year yield is set by long-run inflation expectations, term premium, and fiscal supply — none of which the Fed's short-rate cuts directly address. So households facing a mortgage refinance window, or firms planning long-horizon investment, are not feeling the easing at all. The channels that are working are the short-end ones: variable-rate credit, money-market yields, and the opportunity cost of holding cash. That is a narrower transmission than the conventional model assumes.

**Who is affected differently.** Homeowners with fixed-rate mortgages are largely insulated; they are not repricing. Homeowners with adjustable-rate mortgages, renters facing renewed rent growth, and prospective first-time buyers are exposed. The confidence collapse is concentrated, on past patterns, among lower-income and lower-wealth households, which is consistent with the saving rate falling from 6.2% to 5.5% even as spending holds up — a sign that the marginal household is drawing down buffers. The 12-month change in average hourly earnings (still around 4%) is outpacing CPI (2.3–3.0%), so real wages are rising on paper; but that aggregate number hides a large dispersion between skilled and less-skilled workers, and between those in sectors exposed to AI and automation and those that are not.

**What the conventional reading gets wrong.** The standard interpretation treats the federal funds rate as the price of money for the whole economy. The data above argue against that: the relevant rates for most household and corporate decisions are the long end, and those have not moved with policy. A second error is reading the unemployment rate as a sufficient gauge of labour-market health; at 4.1–4.3% it is unchanged, but the confidence series is telling a different story about forward expectations. A third error is assuming that resilient spending implies a healthy consumer; the falling saving rate and the confidence collapse suggest the resilience may be borrowed or drawn from buffers, which is not sustainable.

**What to test next.** Four hypotheses, each with a specific dataset:

1. *The confidence drop is a one-off shock, not a trend.* Test with the Michigan series' components (current conditions vs. expectations) and the Conference Board Consumer Confidence index; if only expectations are falling and conditions are stable, the shock interpretation gains weight.

2. *Spending resilience is funded by balance-sheet drawdown.* Test with the Fed's Z.1 Financial Accounts (household saving rate, credit-card balances, home-equity line usage) and the Mortgage Bankers Association's refinance and purchase application indices.

3. *The labour market is deteriorating before the headline rate shows it.* Test with JOLTS job openings and quits, initial and continuing claims, and the Challenger layoff tracker; a falling quits rate is the classic leading indicator.

4. *The long-end rates are being held up by fiscal supply rather than inflation expectations.* Test with the 10-year breakeven inflation rate (10Y nominal minus 10Y TIPS) and the term premium estimates from the Cleveland Fed; if the breakeven is falling but the nominal yield is not, the term premium or supply is doing the work.

The story is not yet clear. What is clear is that the transmission mechanism is not working the way the conventional model predicts, and that the confidence data are warning of something the other series have not yet confirmed.
