## The story is not “resilient consumer” versus “high rates”

The table supports a narrower claim: real consumer spending has kept growing, unemployment is low, and real earnings growth is positive, while headline and core inflation have drifted lower and consumer sentiment has fallen sharply. It does not show why. The aggregate numbers are consistent with several stories that have different policy and editorial implications. The feature should be framed around disaggregation: which households, which rates, and which prices.

## Competing explanations

One explanation is that high rates are not very restrictive for the household sector because most existing mortgage debt is fixed at low rates. Fed cuts have not brought the 30-year mortgage rate down much; the table shows mortgage rates near 6.8% even as the funds rate has fallen. That is a transmission story: policy rates affect new borrowing, not outstanding fixed-rate debt. It predicts weak housing turnover and mortgage originations, pressure on renters and first-time buyers, but continued spending by existing owners. It would be distinguished by mortgage-rate distributions, originations, home sales, and spending by housing tenure.

A second explanation is that spending is being carried by labor income and, possibly, wealth effects among higher-income households, while sentiment reflects the price level and borrowing costs. Real earnings growth is positive in the table, and unemployment is low. If this is right, spending should be broad across income groups and should weaken if payroll growth or real disposable income slows. If instead spending is concentrated among high-income or asset-owning households, the “resilient consumer” label is misleading.

A third explanation is a “vibecession”: sentiment has fallen because consumers are angry about cumulative prices and high borrowing costs, not because their current spending capacity has collapsed. The table cannot distinguish sentiment from spending causality. It would be tested with Michigan survey microdata, partisan breakdowns, inflation expectations, and questions on personal finances.

A fourth explanation is that disinflation is mostly goods and supply normalization, while core services remain sticky. Core CPI is lower than a year earlier but still near 3%, and the decline is not monotonic. If services inflation is still being supported by wages and shelter, the Fed’s job is not done. Component-level CPI and PCE data would distinguish this from a broad demand cooling.

## Transmission channels

The mortgage channel is the clearest. The 30-year rate is tied more to the 10-year Treasury and mortgage spreads than to the funds rate. Existing homeowners with low fixed rates are insulated; renters, new buyers, and households needing to move are exposed. That supports spending for some and suppresses housing activity and sentiment for others.

The consumer-credit channel is less visible in the table but important. Credit-card and auto-loan rates are high and often variable. Low-income, young, and lower-wealth households are more likely to borrow at those rates. If delinquencies are rising while aggregate spending holds up, the aggregate is masking stress at the margin.

The saving channel is ambiguous. The personal saving rate is low, which could mean households are spending income and drawing down buffers, or it could reflect measurement and concentration among wealthy households. It does not by itself prove fragility.

The labor-income channel is the main support for spending. Low unemployment and positive real earnings growth can sustain consumption even when sentiment is poor. The wealth channel is not in the table but may matter: high home and equity prices can support spending by owners, especially at the top.

## Who is affected differently

The likely split is between insulated owners and exposed borrowers. Existing fixed-rate homeowners benefit from low legacy rates; renters and first-time buyers face high housing costs. Savers and retirees with cash benefit from higher rates; credit-card borrowers and small businesses face higher costs. High-income households may be supported by income and wealth; lower-income households may feel the price level and credit costs more acutely. The table cannot quantify these groups, but it can motivate the reporting.

## What the conventional reading misses

“Spending is strong, so rates are not biting” is too aggregate. Real spending can grow while housing, small business investment, and lower-income households are under pressure. “Sentiment is collapsing, so recession is coming” is also too simple; sentiment can reflect price levels and news rather than imminent spending cuts. “The Fed has cut, so mortgage relief is coming” misses the long-rate channel. “Inflation is falling, so the Fed can relax” misses sticky core services and the possibility that disinflation is supply-driven.

## What to test next

Testable hypotheses and datasets:

1. **High rates bite marginal borrowers, not existing owners.** Test with HMDA mortgage originations, Black Knight/FHFA mortgage-rate distributions, existing-home sales, housing starts, and NY Fed Household Debt and Credit delinquency data by age and income.
2. **Spending is concentrated among higher-income households.** Test with Consumer Expenditure Survey by income quintile, Opportunity Insights spending by ZIP code, and credit-card data by income tier.
3. **Sentiment reflects price levels and borrowing costs, not labor-market fear.** Test with Michigan survey microdata, partisan and income breakdowns, inflation expectations, and Conference Board confidence.
4. **Disinflation is goods-led; services remain sticky.** Test with CPI/PCE components, shelter and supercore services, producer prices, and wage growth by sector.
5. **Labor income is the spending backstop.** Test with payrolls, real disposable income, JOLTS, claims, and Employment Cost Index.
6. **Fed cuts do not pass through to mortgages.** Test with 10-year term-premium decompositions, mortgage-Treasury spreads, MBS spreads, and Fed funds futures.

The feature should not ask simply why consumers are confident or not. It should ask which consumers are spending, which are borrowing, and which are insulated—and why the policy rate is reaching them so unevenly.
