# The Price of Staying Put

*Headline inflation is rising again, but the squeeze on households isn't just about groceries. It is the result of a price level that has moved permanently higher and a borrowing cost that refuses to come down.*

The University of Michigan’s consumer sentiment index sits at 48. That is a number associated with recessions, not expansions. It is only slightly above the low of 44.8 hit in May 2026, and far below the 71.7 recorded in January 2025. Yet the unemployment rate is 4.1%. In a normal economy, jobs that plentiful would make people feel secure. They do not feel secure.

The disconnect lies in a misunderstanding of what "inflation falling" actually means. For the last year, policymakers and commentators have celebrated a decline in the rate of price increases. That story missed the point. Households do not pay bills with percentages; they pay them with dollars. And the dollar price of the economy has shifted.

Since January 2020, the consumer price index is up 29.8%. That is a permanent change in the cost of living. Average hourly earnings have risen 33.2% over the same period. On paper, the average worker has kept pace. But the aggregate numbers hide a specific, punishing mechanism: the price of money. While wages may have caught up to the price of bread, the cost of borrowing has not gone back. It has gone up.

A household that took out a 30-year fixed mortgage in January 2021 locked in a rate of 2.74%. Today, that rate is 7.33%. This is not a temporary fluctuation; it is a structural shift in the cost of housing and credit. The "inflation is falling" narrative ignored the lock-in effect. People who refinanced at the bottom have little reason to sell, keeping supply tight and prices high. Everyone else is paying a premium to enter the market. So even as the inflation rate cooled, the burden on households intensified. Now, the inflation rate is heating up again, compounding the stress.

The data confirms the re-acceleration. After falling to a low of 2.4% in the 12 months to January 2026, consumer prices rose to 3.7% in the year to September 2026. That is up from 3.0% just a year earlier. The core reading, which strips out food and energy, is 2.6%. This tells us where the pain is coming from: the volatile necessities. The recent surge is concentrated in food and energy, the purchases people notice most often because they make them repeatedly.

This creates a pincer movement. Hourly earnings grew 3.2% in the year to September 2026. That is slower than the 3.7% rise in prices. The average worker is losing purchasing power again. They are not making up the ground lost in previous years; they are falling further behind. Yet the economy continues to move. Real consumer spending is up 2.5% on a year earlier. How is this possible if people are poorer?

They are saving less. The personal saving rate has dropped from 4.9% in October 2025 to 4.1% in October 2026. Households are dipping into their reserves to maintain their standard of living. This is not a sustainable trend. As the University of Michigan researchers noted, "Since mid-2022, consumer sentiment has been moving more closely with sentiment regarding higher prices than with sentiment regarding income." The psychological toll of high prices has decoupled from the mathematical reality of the paycheck.

The mechanism at work is the difference between the stock of wealth and the flow of costs. The cumulative rise in the price level—29.8% since 2020—has already happened. It is done. But the cost of servicing the debt required to live in that more expensive economy is rising. The Federal Reserve held the federal funds rate at 3.63% through August 2026, but it has since raised it to 3.88%. That increase feeds directly into mortgage rates and auto loans.

Mark Zandi of Moody’s Analytics pointed to housing as a primary driver. "Perhaps the largest contributor to the outsized inflation reading in Tuesday's CPI report was housing, Zandi said." This creates a feedback loop. High interest rates make it expensive to buy a home, which keeps people in rentals or in their current homes, which props up the cost of shelter, which keeps inflation elevated. The household stuck renting or looking to buy is hit twice: once by the high price level, and again by the high cost of the mortgage needed to pay it.

Consumers are adapting, but not in ways that suggest confidence. Research shows that "Primarily, consumers started trading down, but as inflation’s impact on consumers persisted, broader behaviors started to change; these included less frequent purchases, shifting from restaurants to eating at home." This is the behavior of a population trying to outrun a rising tide by cutting costs, not one benefiting from a booming labor market.

The outlook hinges on whether this re-acceleration is temporary or structural. If the rise in headline inflation to 3.7% persists, the Federal Reserve will be forced to keep interest rates higher for longer. A federal funds rate of 3.88% is already restrictive compared to the 3.63% floor of earlier this year. If it climbs further, the 7.33% mortgage rate could look like a bargain in hindsight.

The critical variable to watch is not the unemployment rate, which is likely to remain low, but the saving rate. At 4.1%, the buffer is thin. If households continue to spend by draining savings, the economy can muddle through. But if the saving rate hits a floor, spending will have to contract. The "soft landing" scenario assumes that the pain of high prices is manageable because wages are high. The data suggests the pain is concentrated in the cost of credit, which wages cannot fix.

We are not in a period of "transitory" inflation anymore. We are in a period of high costs. The 2.74% mortgage rate is gone. The 29.8% price increase is baked in. The only question left is how long households can keep spending money they are no longer saving.
