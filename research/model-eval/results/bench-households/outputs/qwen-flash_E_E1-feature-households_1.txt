# The Rate Fell, the Price Didn’t

*The inflation rate has eased, but the cumulative cost of living has not. As headline prices tick back up, the gap between what households earn and what they pay to borrow is widening again.*

The story of American inflation over the last three years has been told as a victory of mathematics over memory. The rate of price growth peaked, wobbled, and then fell. By January 2026, the consumer price index (CPI) had dropped to 2.4% for the preceding twelve months, a number that sounded like normalcy. If you looked only at the dashboard, the crisis appeared to be over.

But households do not live on the dashboard. They live in the cumulative reality of what things cost. Since January 2020, the CPI price level has risen 29.8%. Average hourly earnings have risen 33.2% over the same period. On paper, the average worker has more than kept pace with the price of a basket of goods. Yet the prevailing mood is not one of relief. It is one of exhaustion. The disconnect between the statistical "win" and the lived experience of being poorer is not a paradox; it is a matter of which numbers matter to a family trying to buy a home or pay a credit card bill.

The primary driver of this disconnect is not the grocery bill, though food prices are volatile. It is the cost of capital. When the Federal Reserve slashed rates to support the economy during the pandemic, the 30-year fixed mortgage rate hit 2.74% in January 2021. That rate is gone. In October 2026, the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate stood at 7.33%. A household that refinanced or bought at the bottom has little reason to sell, locking in low costs for themselves while pricing out everyone else. For those without that equity cushion, the barrier to entry has doubled.

This is where the "inflation is falling" narrative breaks down. While the rate of price growth slowed, the level of prices remained high, and the cost of borrowing to finance those prices surged. The Federal Reserve held its key interest rate at 3.63% through August 2026 before raising it to 3.88% in October, a move that ripples through every loan product in the economy. The squeeze people describe is less about the marginal cost of milk and more about the absolute cost of shelter and credit.

And now, the rate of price growth is turning up again. In September 2026, CPI rose 3.7% compared with a year earlier, up from 3.0% in September 2025. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, sits at 2.6%, but the headline number is what hits the wallet. The recent acceleration is concentrated in the categories consumers notice most. "Perhaps the largest contributor to the outsized inflation reading in Tuesday's CPI report was housing, Zandi said," Mark Zandi of Moody’s Analytics told CNBC. When housing costs are the primary driver of inflation, the pain is not abstract; it is structural.

The labor market, often the buffer against economic shocks, is no longer providing that cushion for the average worker. Average hourly earnings grew 3.2% in the twelve months to September 2026. With inflation at 3.7%, the average worker’s pay is losing purchasing power in real terms. For the first time since the initial shock of 2022, the trend is negative. The "wage-price spiral" is not back in the sense of runaway increases, but the wage-price gap has reopened.

Households are not retreating from spending. Real consumer spending rose 2.5% in the twelve months to October 2026. But look at the funding source. The personal saving rate fell from 4.9% in October 2025 to 4.1% in October 2026. This suggests that consumption is being sustained by drawing down savings rather than by income growth. This is a finite resource. When the buffer runs thin, spending behavior changes. "Primarily, consumers started trading down, but as inflation’s impact on consumers persisted, broader behaviors started to change; these included less frequent purchases, shifting from restaurants to eating at home," according to a consumer-behaviour research note. The shift is not just in what people buy, but in how often they buy it.

The psychological toll is visible in the sentiment data. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index stood at 48 in October 2026, far below the 71.7 recorded in January 2025. It had dipped to 44.8 in May 2026, the lowest reading in the available data since at least 2016. This weakness persists despite an unemployment rate of 4.1%, which is historically low. The data suggests that job security is no longer the primary metric of consumer confidence. "Since mid-2022, consumer sentiment has been moving more closely with sentiment regarding higher prices than with sentiment regarding income," the University of Michigan noted in its Surveys of Consumers.

This distinction is crucial for understanding the current economic tension. A low unemployment rate signals strength in the labor market, but it does not signal affordability in the asset market. The average worker has a job, but they also have a mortgage payment that may be nearly three times what it would have been in 2021, and a grocery bill that is nearly 30% higher than it was pre-pandemic. The cumulative rise in the CPI price level since January 2020 is a 29.8% tax on cash holdings, eroding the value of savings even as nominal balances remain stable.

The re-acceleration of inflation to 3.7% complicates the path forward. If the Federal Reserve continues to raise rates to combat this uptick, borrowing costs will climb further, deepening the divide between asset owners and non-owners. If they pause, the risk is that inflation expectations become unanchored, leading to a wage-price feedback loop that erodes the 3.2% earnings growth even further.

The mechanism is clear: the lock-in of past borrowing costs into the price of anything bought on credit. Those who bought homes or cars at the low rates of 2021 are insulated. Those who did not are exposed to the full force of the 7.33% mortgage rate and the 3.88% federal funds rate. This is not a story of a recession in the traditional sense, with mass layoffs and collapsing output. It is a story of bifurcation, where the economy grows (real spending is up 2.5%) but the distribution of pain becomes more acute.

The saving rate decline from 4.9% to 4.1% is the most fragile thread in this picture. It indicates that households are stretching to maintain their standard of living. If inflation persists at 3.7% or higher, and if earnings growth remains at 3.2%, that gap must be closed either by cutting spending or by depleting savings. Neither is sustainable indefinitely.

What to watch is not the unemployment rate, which will likely remain resilient, but the spread between earnings growth and the cost of credit. The signpost is the 30-year mortgage rate. If it holds above 7%, the lock-in effect will continue to suppress supply and keep housing unaffordable for new entrants, regardless of how low the inflation rate looks on a year-over-year basis. The "rate" may fall again, but the "price" is unlikely to return to 2020 levels. The household balance sheet, not the GDP report, is where the next phase of this economic adjustment will be decided.
