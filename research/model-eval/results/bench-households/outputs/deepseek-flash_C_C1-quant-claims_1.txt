**C1.** Not supported as stated. The mortgage rate did fall from 6.92% (June 2024) to 6.18% (September), but most of that move came *before* the Fed cut: the 10-year yield fell from 4.31% to 3.72% between June and September, while the funds rate was unchanged until September. After the cuts began, mortgage rates rose again (6.43% in October, 6.96% by January 2025) even as the funds rate kept falling. The change correlations confirm this: mortgage changes track 10-year yield changes (r = 0.83) far more than funds-rate changes (r = 0.16). The 0.93 level correlation is a spurious trend artifact — both series drifted over the period. Confounders: expectations, term premia, inflation news. Further analysis: event-study around FOMC dates, controlling for the 10-year yield.

**C2.** Not supported. The 0.94 is a correlation of *levels*, which is dominated by shared upward trends (both CPI and stock prices rose over the decade); it says nothing about whether stocks hedge inflation. The change correlation, r = -0.01, is the relevant statistic and shows essentially no relationship between monthly CPI moves and monthly stock returns. If anything, the near-zero change correlation undercuts the "proven hedge" claim. Confounders: trend, earnings growth, real vs nominal. Further analysis: regress real stock returns on inflation surprises, test across subperiods and inflation regimes, and check longer horizons.

**C3.** Not supported. The sample contains a single inversion episode, so "perfect record" is a claim about n = 1 — no basis for a rule. The data also don't show a clear recession: unemployment rose from 3.4% to 4.2% by the end of the inversion and to 4.5% by late 2025, then fell to 4.1% by October 2026; payrolls rose throughout (153.1m → 157.8m → 159.3m). A modest labor-market cooling is visible, not a recession. Confounders: the sample period, definition of recession, lag structure. Further analysis: multiple historical episodes, formal recession dating, and lag/lead tests between spread and activity.

**C4.** Not supported as a "fixed rule." The two windows give opposite signs: r = 0.07 (2017–19, essentially no relationship) and r = -0.5 (2022–24, a moderate negative one). A rule that flips sign across periods isn't a rule. The 2022–24 negative correlation is consistent with rate-driven valuation compression, but that regime may not generalize. Confounders: inflation regime, growth expectations, sector composition. Further analysis: longer samples, regime-switching models, controlling for growth and inflation surprises.

**C5.** Insufficient evidence. With n = 6, r = -0.16 is trivially small and statistically meaningless; six monthly observations cannot establish that sentiment and spending "moved in opposite directions." The claim overreads noise. Confounders: small sample, timing lags, income and credit conditions. Further analysis: a multi-year sample, lagged correlations, and controls for income and interest rates.

**C6.** Not supported as stated. The 0.78 is driven by the March–July 2020 collapse and rebound, when both payrolls and spending fell and recovered together. Excluding those months, r = 0.27 — a weak relationship. The headline correlation mostly measures one extreme episode, not a general "jobs drive spending" mechanism. Confounders: the pandemic shock, common cyclical factors, measurement timing. Further analysis: exclude outliers, use rolling windows, and test with lagged payroll changes and controls for income and confidence.
