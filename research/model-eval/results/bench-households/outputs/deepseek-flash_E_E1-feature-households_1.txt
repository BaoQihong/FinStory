# The inflation rate fell. The price of everything else didn't.

*Households were told the worst was over because the rate of price rises came down. They kept paying the higher prices, and now the cost of borrowing against them is going up again.*

In January 2026, inflation hit 2.4%, the low point of the past year. By the standards of the previous four years, that counted as victory, and it was reported as one. Nine months later, consumer prices were rising at 3.7% over twelve months, up from 3.0% a year earlier. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, was 2.6%. The recent turn is concentrated in the things people buy most often and notice most: groceries and petrol.

Here is the confusion at the heart of the past four years. "Inflation is falling" was always a statement about a rate, and households live with a level. Since January 2020, the consumer price index has risen 29.8%. Average hourly earnings are up 33.2% over the same period. On average, pay has more than kept pace with prices. That is the arithmetic, and it is true. It is also not what the mood of the country says.

The University of Michigan's consumer sentiment index stood at 48 in October 2026. In January 2025 it was 71.7; its low point, in May 2026, was 44.8. Unemployment is 4.1%. This is not a story about people losing their jobs. It is a story about people who have jobs, and incomes, and who nonetheless feel worse off than they did.

The surveys offer a clue. "Since mid-2022, consumer sentiment has been moving more closely with sentiment regarding higher prices than with sentiment regarding income," the Michigan researchers wrote. Households are not grading their pay rise against their old pay. They are grading it against the price level they now face every week at the till.

But the grocery bill is not the whole of it, and possibly not the largest part. The price of borrowing has not come back down with the rate of inflation. A household that took out a 30-year fixed mortgage in January 2021 did so at 2.74%. By October 2026 the same mortgage cost 7.33%, up from 6.25% a year earlier. The Federal Reserve held its policy rate at 3.63% from May to August 2026 and has since raised it to 3.88%.

That gap matters more than it sounds. Most households do not refinance every year, so the mortgage rate that governs their life is the one they signed. But anyone trying to buy a first home, move, or borrow against the value of the one they have is now paying a rate set in a different world. The lock-in cuts both ways: existing owners with cheap mortgages have little reason to sell, which keeps the price of the houses that are for sale high, while the cost of the loan needed to buy one has more than doubled. The squeeze people describe is less about the loaf of bread than about the ladder into housing and credit, and how much further away the first rung has moved.

The spending data says households are not, in fact, pulling back. Real consumer spending rose 2.5% in the twelve months to October 2026. But the personal saving rate fell from 4.9% in October 2025 to 4.1% in October 2026. Some of that spending is being paid for by saving less. That can run for a while. It cannot run forever.

The mechanism is straightforward once you separate the two ideas that share the word "inflation". The rate is the speed at which prices change; the level is where they have ended up. A falling rate means prices are rising more slowly, not that they are coming down. Wages can outrun the level over six years and still lose ground in any given year, which is what happened: in the twelve months to September 2026, average hourly earnings grew 3.2% while prices rose 3.7%. The average worker's pay is again losing purchasing power, just as the earlier catch-up was starting to be felt.

Which is why the recent rise in headline inflation matters more than its size suggests. A 3.7% reading is not 2022. But it arrives at the moment households had begun to hope the level would stop moving. The Michigan data suggests sentiment tracks prices, not income, so a renewed rise in the price of food and fuel lands directly on the mood.

There are limits to what this evidence can tell us. It does not break households down by income, or separate renters from owners, so we cannot say which families are hit hardest. It does not prove that mortgage rates cause the sentiment slump; the correlation is suggestive, not causal. And it cannot say whether the 2026 rise in headline inflation will persist or fade, as the 2025 spike did. The research desk's reading is that the rise is concentrated in food and energy, which are volatile, and that core inflation at 2.6% is the better guide to the underlying trend. But that is an interpretation, not a fact.

The consumer-behaviour research offers one more signal. "Primarily, consumers started trading down, but as inflation's impact on consumers persisted, broader behaviors started to change; these included less frequent purchases, shifting from restaurants to eating at home," the note reads. Trading down is what people do when they believe the price level is permanent. It is a quiet admission that the old prices are not coming back.

What to watch is not the headline rate alone but the gap between it and earnings. If pay growth stays below price growth, the average worker keeps losing ground, and the saving rate has further to fall before spending follows it down. If the Federal Reserve keeps raising rates to contain the renewed inflation, the mortgage arithmetic gets worse for anyone who needs credit. The sentiment index will tell us which of those the country is living through. It has already told us that the rate of inflation was never the number households were watching.
