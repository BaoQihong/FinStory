## Part 1 — Interpretation

The table describes a household sector that is, on the surface, still employed and still spending, but increasingly squeezed and increasingly pessimistic. The most important distinction is between levels and rates of change: real consumption spending and payroll employment are at or near their highest levels in the sample, yet the rates of change in real incomes and sentiment have deteriorated.

**Labour market and income.** Payrolls rose from 157,912 thousand in 2024-09 to 159,293 thousand in 2026-10, an increase of about 1.38 million, or roughly 0.9% over 25 months — a slow pace. The unemployment rate drifted up from 4.0–4.1% to 4.4–4.5% (a rise of roughly 0.3–0.4 percentage points, not percent). Average hourly earnings rose from $35.34 to $38.00, about 7.5% over the period. But CPI rose from 314.732 to 337.476, about 7.2%. So nominal wage gains have only barely outpaced headline inflation over the full period, and the margin has narrowed sharply at the end: in the 12 months to 2026-09, wages rose about 3.2% while CPI rose about 3.7%, implying a small fall in real earnings. The 2026-03 CPI jump (319.785 to 330.293 in one month, +3.3%) is a striking outlier and is the main driver of the late-period inflation pickup.

**Spending and saving.** Real PCE rose steadily from $16,165bn to $17,003bn (about 5.2%), so households kept spending in volume terms. But the personal saving rate fell from 5.7% to 4.1%, and to 3.6% in 2026-09 — the lowest in the table. Falling saving alongside rising spending is consistent with households drawing down buffers or leaning on credit; the data cannot tell us which.

**Sentiment and rates.** Consumer sentiment collapsed from 70.1 to a low of 44.8 in 2026-05, then recovered only partially to 48.0. Meanwhile the effective fed funds rate fell from 5.13% to 3.88% (a cut of 125 basis points), yet the 30-year mortgage rate rose from 6.18% to 7.33%, and from 6.25% in 2025-10 to 7.33% in 2026-10. That divergence — easier policy, tighter long-term borrowing costs — is a meaningful signal that long-rate expectations, not policy, are driving housing affordability.

**Limitations.** These are aggregate, seasonally adjusted series. They say nothing about the distribution of gains across households, debt service burdens, rents versus owner costs, or wealth. The CPI is an index, not a cost-of-living measure for any particular family. Sentiment is a survey of perceptions, not measured hardship. And 2025-10 CPI, core CPI and unemployment are missing, so month-to-month comparisons spanning that gap cannot be made.
