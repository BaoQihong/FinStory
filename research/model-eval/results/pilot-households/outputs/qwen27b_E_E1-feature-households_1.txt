# The Price Level Is the Problem, Not the Rate

Households feel poorer not because prices are falling too slowly, but because the cost of living has permanently reset. The recent acceleration in inflation is the wrong headline; the real story is that the price of borrowing has not gone back, trapping families in a squeeze that wage growth alone cannot fix.

For the last few years, the dominant narrative in financial media has been one of relief. Inflation, that chaotic force that erodes savings and complicates budgeting, was finally coming down. It hit a low point of 2.4% in the twelve months to January 2026. The story went that the worst was behind us. But that story was about the rate of change, not the level. It is a distinction that matters deeply to anyone paying for a home, a car, or a college degree, because the "rate" is a speedometer, while the "level" is the odometer. You can slow down, but the miles you have already driven do not disappear.

Since January 2020, the Consumer Price Index (CPI) price level has risen by 29.8%. In the same period, average hourly earnings have risen by 33.2%. On paper, it looks like a win. Wages have outpaced prices, preserving and even slightly expanding purchasing power for the average worker. This is the part of the data that supports the "recovery" narrative. But this aggregate average hides a critical structural shift: the cost of accessing credit.

Consider the housing market. In January 2021, a family could lock in a 30-year fixed mortgage at 2.74%. That was cheap money, almost free by historical standards. Today, in October 2026, that same 30-year rate sits at 7.33%. The difference is not a marginal adjustment; it is a fundamental change in the cost of entry. A household that refinanced or bought at the bottom has little reason to sell, but a household trying to buy now faces a monthly payment burden that is structurally higher than it was five years ago. The price of the house may not have quadrupled, but the price of the *loan* has.

This is where the feeling of being "squeezed" comes from, and it is not just about the grocery bill. It is about the ladder. When you are on the ladder, you are paying a rate that reflects the market of the day you climbed. If the market moves against you, you are locked in. But if you are trying to climb, you are paying the current, higher price. The gap between the cost of existing debt and the cost of new debt creates a wedge in the economy. People who own assets bought cheaply are insulated; people who need new credit are not.

The recent data confirms that this tension is tightening again. Headline inflation, which had dipped to 3.0% in the twelve months to September 2025, has re-accelerated to 3.7% by September 2026. Core inflation, which excludes the volatile food and energy components, is at 2.6%. The fact that headline inflation is running higher than core suggests that the pain is concentrated in the items people notice most often: the gas pump, the supermarket, the utility bill. These are the daily reminders that the price level is still rising.

Wages are not keeping up with this new velocity. Average hourly earnings grew by 3.2% in the year to September 2026. That is slower than the 3.7% rise in prices. For the first time in a while, the average worker is losing purchasing power, not just holding it steady. This is a subtle but important shift. Previously, the worry was that wages would stall. Now, the worry is that they are shrinking in real terms because the price level is moving faster than the paycheck.

The Federal Reserve is watching this closely. The central bank held its federal funds rate at 3.63% through August 2026, but has since raised it to 3.88%. This is a signal that the Fed sees inflation as sticky, not transient. They are not trying to crush the economy, but they are refusing to pretend that the price level has stabilized. Higher policy rates keep mortgage rates elevated. The 30-year mortgage rate was 6.25% in October 2025; it is now 7.33%. That one-point increase is the difference between a manageable payment and a prohibitive one for many buyers.

So, how are households coping? They are spending, but they are spending by saving less. Real consumer spending, adjusted for inflation, is up 2.5% in the twelve months to October 2026. That sounds healthy. But the personal saving rate, the share of disposable income that is not spent, has fallen from 4.9% in October 2025 to 4.1% in October 2026. Households are financing their consumption by dipping into their safety nets. They are trading future security for present comfort. This is a fragile equilibrium. It works until it doesn't. It works until a medical bill, a car repair, or a job loss turns that reduced cushion into a crisis.

The sentiment data tells the same story from a different angle. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index stood at 48 in October 2026. For context, it was 71.7 in January 2025. It hit a low of 44.8 in May 2026, the weakest reading in our data since at least 2016. This is not a story about fear of unemployment. The unemployment rate is 4.1%, which is moderate, not high. People are not out of work; they are working. They are just poorer relative to the cost of the things they need.

The University of Michigan notes that "Since mid-2022, consumer sentiment has been moving more closely with sentiment regarding higher prices than with sentiment regarding income." This is a key insight. It means that when people feel bad, it is not because they think they will lose their jobs. It is because they think their money is worth less. The psychological burden of inflation is not about the risk of job loss; it is about the erosion of the value of the work you are already doing.

Mark Zandi of Moody’s Analytics pointed out that "Perhaps the largest contributor to the outsized inflation reading in Tuesday's CPI report was housing." This makes sense. Housing is not just the roof over your head; it is the biggest asset most families own and the biggest line item in their budget. When the cost of housing services rises, it drags the entire price level up. And because housing is so sticky, it does not come down quickly. It lingers.

What happens now? The evidence does not tell us. We do not know if the rise in headline inflation is a one-off blip or the start of a new cycle. We do not know if the Fed will raise rates further or hold. What we do know is that the buffer is gone. The saving rate is low. The price level is high. The cost of new credit is high. The average worker is seeing their real pay shrink.

This is not a situation that will resolve itself with a few months of stable prices. It is a structural adjustment. The economy has moved to a higher price level, and the costs of borrowing have moved with it. The "inflation is falling" story was a temporary reprieve, not a permanent cure. The real test is not whether the rate of inflation drops to 2%. The real test is whether wages can rise fast enough to make the 7.33% mortgage rate feel normal again, or whether the gap between the cost of living and the cost of credit will keep widening.

For now, the squeeze is real. It is not a perception; it is a calculation. And it is one that will not disappear just because the news cycle moves on to the next quarterly earnings report. The price level is where it is. The rate of change is less important than the distance traveled. And the distance traveled is long.
