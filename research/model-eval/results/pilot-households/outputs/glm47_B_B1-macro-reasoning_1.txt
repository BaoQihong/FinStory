**The Dislocation: Why Spending Holds While Confidence Collapses**

The economic data from mid-2024 through mid-2025 presents a genuine puzzle for market participants and policymakers. We are witnessing a rare divergence: real consumer spending is growing at a respectable clip—hovering near 3% for much of the period—while the University of Michigan’s consumer sentiment index has plummeted from the mid-70s to the low-50s. Simultaneously, inflation has retreated toward target, yet long-term interest rates have risen, not fallen, even as the Federal Reserve has eased the federal funds rate.

This combination suggests that the economy is not merely slowing; it is undergoing a structural shift in how households experience financial stress. The conventional narrative—that lower inflation automatically boosts confidence and that rate cuts automatically ease financial conditions—is clearly insufficient. To commission a compelling feature, we must look beyond the aggregate numbers and examine the specific channels through which policy is transmitting—or failing to transmit—into the real economy.

**Competing Explanations: The "Wealth Effect" vs. The "Debt Trap"**

Two primary competing explanations emerge from this dataset. The first is the **"Wealth Effect" hypothesis**. Despite the drop in sentiment, households continue to spend because their balance sheets remain robust. The data shows the personal saving rate dipping to 5.1% in December 2024 and hovering near 5.5% to 6.2% since. This suggests that consumers are dipping into accumulated savings or relying on asset appreciation to maintain consumption. Under this view, the drop in sentiment reflects "noise"—perhaps political anxiety or media pessimism—rather than a genuine economic constraint.

The second explanation is the **"Debt Trap" hypothesis**. Here, the divergence is explained by the bifurcation of the consumer base. A cohort of households with fixed-rate mortgages (locked in years ago) and substantial savings is spending freely, keeping the aggregate "Real consumer spending" figure high. However, a marginal cohort is being squeezed by elevated borrowing costs, causing the sentiment collapse. The sentiment index might be capturing the distress of this marginal group more acutely than the spending data, which is skewed by the top earners.

To distinguish between these, we must look at the relationship between interest rates and the cost of capital. The data shows that while the Effective Federal Funds Rate fell from 5.33% to 4.33%, the 30-year fixed mortgage rate actually rose from 6.5% to 6.82% between August 2024 and June 2025. The 10-year Treasury yield followed a similar upward path. If the "Wealth Effect" were dominant, one would expect lower short-term rates to support asset prices and borrowing. Instead, rising long-term rates suggest the market is pricing in a different reality—one where the "Debt Trap" may be tightening its grip.

**Channels of Transmission: The Broken Ladder**

The most critical story hidden in this table is the breakdown of the traditional monetary policy transmission channel. Typically, a reduction in the federal funds rate leads to lower yields across the curve and cheaper mortgages. Here, we see the opposite: the "anchor" has dropped, but the "boat" (long-term rates) has floated higher.

This divergence implies that the Fed's policy rate is losing its influence over the borrowing costs that matter most to households. The 30-year mortgage rate is rising despite the Fed's cuts, likely due to term premiums (investors demanding more compensation for holding long-term debt) or expectations of future inflation resurgence. Consequently, the housing market—a primary driver of consumer wealth—remains tight. The channel through which rate cuts usually stimulate the economy (refinancing and home equity extraction) is effectively clogged.

Furthermore, the transmission to the labor market appears delayed. Average hourly earnings growth remains sticky, hovering between 3.86% and 4.21% year-over-year, well above the 2.3% to 2.9% CPI inflation readings. This "real wage growth" explains why spending hasn't collapsed. Workers are earning more in real terms than they were a year ago. However, the unemployment rate has ticked up to 4.2% in recent months, suggesting that the labor market is cooling. The channel here is lagged: workers are currently spending their nominal raises, but as unemployment creeps up, the sustainability of that spending comes into question.

**Distributional Impacts: The Fixed-Rate Divide**

The data highlights a stark distributional asymmetry. The impact of elevated rates is not felt evenly. Households with existing fixed-rate debt are insulated from the rise in 10-year yields and mortgage rates. Their monthly payments are unchanged, and their real wages have increased. They are the engine driving the 3%+ growth in real spending.

Conversely, prospective homebuyers, renters, and small businesses relying on commercial loans are facing a tightening financial environment. The rise in the 10-year Treasury yield to 4.38% in June 2025 increases the cost of capital for everyone who *needs* to borrow now. This group is likely driving the collapse in consumer sentiment. They are priced out of the housing market and face higher costs for auto loans and credit cards. The aggregate spending data masks this pain because the "insulated" majority has a higher marginal propensity to consume in the short term than the "priced-out" minority has to cut back.

**What the Conventional Reading Misses**

The conventional reading of this data would be that the economy is "soft landing"—inflation is down, spending is up, and the Fed is cutting rates. This view misses the growing fragility in the financial markets. It misses the fact that the yield curve is steepening (long rates rising while short rates fall) in a way that suggests the market is fighting the Fed.

A standard analysis might also dismiss the sentiment drop as irrelevant given the spending data. This is a mistake. Sentiment is often a leading indicator for labor market churn. If workers feel insecure, they may reduce hours or stop job-hopping, which eventually impacts wage growth. The conventional view treats the consumer as a monolith; the reality is a tale of two economies.

**Testable Hypotheses and Required Data**

To verify these inferences, I would commission the feature to test the following hypotheses:

1.  **Hypothesis 1:** The rise in real consumer spending is being driven by upper-income households with fixed-rate mortgages, while lower-income households are reducing consumption.
    *   **Required Data:** We need distributional real Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) data by income quintile, alongside data on the savings rates by income bracket. This would show if the "saving rate" decline is concentrated at the top.

2.  **Hypothesis 2:** The rise in long-term yields is driven by a supply shock (increased government issuance) rather than inflation expectations.
    *   **Required Data:** We need data on Treasury auction sizes and foreign holdings of Treasuries, combined with 5-year, 5-year forward inflation expectations. If inflation expectations are anchored but yields are rising, the "supply shock" theory is confirmed.

3.  **Hypothesis 3:** Consumer sentiment is a leading indicator for a rise in the unemployment rate, breaking the current correlation between low unemployment and high spending.
    *   **Required Data:** We would look at the Conference Board’s Labor Market Differential index (jobs hard to get vs. jobs plentiful) and correlate it with the University of Michigan data to see if the sentiment drop is specifically employment-driven.

The story is not about a stable economy; it is about an economy that is re-pricing risk. The divergence between the "hard data" of spending and the "soft data" of sentiment is the friction point where the next economic shift will likely occur.
