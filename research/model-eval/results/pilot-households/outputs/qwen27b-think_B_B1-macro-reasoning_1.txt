Full draft:

**The puzzle, not the table**

The combination is unusual because the usual recession template—rising unemployment, falling wages, collapsing spending—has not appeared, while the usual confidence template—current conditions deteriorating—has. The evidence shows disinflation, a stable labor market, positive real spending, and weak sentiment. The inference to draw is that confidence may be responding to something other than current aggregate income: uncertainty, borrowing costs, or distributional stress.

**Competing explanations**

The first is a soft landing with an uncertainty drag. Inflation is easing, wages are still growing faster than prices, and spending remains positive. Confidence could be falling because households are reacting to policy uncertainty, fiscal concerns, trade or geopolitical shocks, or the risk that the soft landing fails. The distinguishing feature would be weak expectations but stable current conditions, with spending holding up until uncertainty resolves.

The second is concentrated rate pass-through. Elevated policy rates may be suppressing rate-sensitive activity—housing, construction, variable-rate borrowing, and investment—without yet showing up in aggregate consumption. This is plausible because mortgage rates remain high even when Treasury yields move, and because fixed-rate mortgages make the transmission indirect. The distinguishing feature would be weakness in housing and credit, with non-housing spending relatively resilient.

The third is a distributional squeeze. Aggregate CPI and average hourly earnings can look benign while lower-income households face heavier effective inflation from shelter, insurance, healthcare, food, and debt service. Confidence may be reflecting balance-sheet stress that is invisible in national averages. The distinguishing feature would be divergence across income groups, regions, renter/owner status, and spending categories.

The fourth is early demand destruction. Confidence may be a leading indicator of a spending slowdown that has not yet reached the labor market. The distinguishing feature would be falling savings, weaker credit demand, rising delinquencies, and a labor market that begins to loosen.

The fifth is a fiscal or term-premium story. Long rates can stay elevated even after policy cuts if markets price higher fiscal supply, term risk, or policy uncertainty. That raises borrowing costs for households and firms and can depress confidence independently of the federal funds rate. The distinguishing feature would be wide term spreads, weak Treasury auction demand, and rising credit spreads.

**Channels**

Policy rates reach the economy through several routes. They affect short-term funding, bank reserves, credit spreads, and the term structure. For households, the direct channels are new borrowing, variable-rate debt, and the cost of refinancing. Fixed-rate mortgage holders do not see their payments rise, but they face a high opportunity cost: they cannot refinance cheaply, housing turnover falls, and construction and developer investment are constrained. Higher rates also affect asset prices and wealth, especially equities and housing.

The channels are not uniform. A household with a low fixed mortgage may feel protected, while a renter, new buyer, or credit-card borrower feels the rate increase immediately. A saver may benefit from higher yields, while a leveraged household is squeezed. The data do not show which channel is dominant; they only show that aggregate spending has not collapsed.

**Who is affected differently**

Fixed-rate homeowners are insulated from payment shocks but locked out of mobility. Renters and new buyers face higher effective housing costs. Variable-rate borrowers—credit cards, HELOCs, some auto loans—face higher debt service. Lower-income households typically spend a larger share of income on essentials and housing, so a given CPI change can matter more. Higher-income households may be supported by wage growth, asset income, and savings. Firms with floating-rate debt, real estate developers, and rate-sensitive investors are exposed differently from firms with fixed-rate debt.

**What the conventional reading misses**

The conventional reading is that high rates plus low confidence imply recession, or that falling inflation means the economy is fine. Both are incomplete. Confidence can be driven by uncertainty rather than current hardship. CPI can understate the cost pressure felt by specific households. Policy rates are not the only relevant rate; mortgage rates, credit spreads, and term premia matter. Twelve-month changes also smooth short-term acceleration, so a stable unemployment rate can coexist with a weakening labor market.

**Tests**

I would test five hypotheses.

First, is confidence uncertainty-driven? Compare Michigan current conditions with expectations, add the NY Fed Survey of Consumer Expectations, and overlay policy uncertainty or news indicators. If uncertainty is the driver, expectations should fall while current conditions remain stable and spending should not collapse.

Second, is housing the main drag? Use MBA mortgage applications, refinance index, NAR existing-home sales, Census new-home sales and starts, FHFA home prices, and construction data. If the rate channel is concentrated in housing, housing activity should be weak while non-housing spending remains resilient.

Third, is credit tightening? Use the NY Fed Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey, consumer credit data, delinquency rates, credit-card and auto rates, and loan volumes. If credit is the channel, standards should tighten and delinquencies should rise, especially among lower-income borrowers.

Fourth, is the squeeze distributional? Use BEA personal consumption by income, CPI by category, CPS income and housing data, Federal Reserve Flow of Funds, and debt-service-to-income measures. If distribution matters, spending should diverge by category and income group even if aggregate PCE is stable.

Fifth, is fiscal or term risk raising long rates? Use Treasury constant-maturity spreads, auction tails, credit spreads, and fiscal data. If fiscal supply or term premium is the issue, long rates should rise even as policy rates fall, and confidence should move with policy uncertainty rather than current conditions.

The story should not be “recession” or “all clear.” It should be a diagnosis of which channel is doing the work: uncertainty, housing, credit, distribution, or fiscal risk.

**Competing explanations**

The distinguishing feature would be weak expectations but stable current conditions, with spending holding up until uncertainty resolves.
