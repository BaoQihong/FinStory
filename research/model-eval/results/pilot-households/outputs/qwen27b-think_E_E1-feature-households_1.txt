# The inflation story was never just about the rate
*Households live with price levels, not percentages. With borrowing costs still far above the early 2020s, the squeeze is showing up in savings, sentiment and the housing ladder.*

A 30-year fixed mortgage rate of 2.74% in January 2021 is a number that matters less in the news than it does in a life plan. It was the price of a long-term home loan at a moment when many households could borrow cheaply. By October 2026 the same rate was 7.33%. That gap, more than the latest inflation print, helps explain why so many people feel poorer even when the rate of inflation falls.

The official inflation story has been about momentum. Consumer prices rose 3.7% in the 12 months to September 2026, up from 3.0% a year earlier. Earlier in the year, the rate had dipped to 2.4% in the 12 months to January 2026, the low point of the last year. That is the kind of movement that makes a good chart: inflation cooling, then picking up again. But households do not budget by percentage changes. They live with the level.

Between January 2020 and September 2026, the CPI price level rose 29.8%. Average hourly earnings rose 33.2% over the same stretch. On average, pay has more than kept pace with prices. That is not a comforting thought for a renter, a first-time buyer, or a household trying to move into a bigger home, because the price of credit has not gone back with it. The mortgage rate that was 2.74% in January 2021 was 6.25% in October 2025 and 7.33% in October 2026. A household that refinanced at the bottom has little reason to sell, which is one reason the housing market can feel frozen even while prices remain high.

Our reading is that the squeeze people describe is less about the grocery bill alone than about the cost of getting onto, or moving up, the housing and credit ladder. Inflation makes the groceries, gas and utility bill more visible. Borrowing costs make the house, the car and the business loan harder to reach. When the two move together, the mood sours.

That is the part most coverage misses. Headline inflation is a rate: the speed at which prices are changing. The price level is the accumulated result of all those changes. A person who has been watching the news for two years has been hearing that inflation is falling, then rising, then falling again. But the price of a gallon of milk, a loaf of bread, a new phone, a delivery meal and a rental apartment has not reverted to its 2020 level. It has been added to, month after month.

The latest data show the re-acceleration is concentrated in the purchases people notice most often. Core inflation, excluding food and energy, was 2.6% in the 12 months to September 2026. Headline inflation was 3.7%. The gap matters: it means food and energy are doing a lot of the work in the current print. That is also why the emotional response is strong. People buy groceries weekly and fill the tank regularly. They do not buy a house every year, but they feel every month that the house is farther away.

The labour market is not the obvious explanation. Unemployment was 4.1% in October 2026. That is not a jobs crisis. Average hourly earnings grew 3.2% in the year to September 2026, which is slower than prices, so the average worker's pay is again losing purchasing power. But the sentiment data are far weaker than the jobs data. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index was 48 in October 2026, against 71.7 in January 2025 and a low of 44.8 in May 2026. The University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers put it plainly: "Since mid-2022, consumer sentiment has been moving more closely with sentiment regarding higher prices than with sentiment regarding income."

That quote is useful because it separates two things that usually move together: income and prices. If people feel poorer because they are earning less, the policy conversation is about jobs. If they feel poorer because prices have risen, the conversation is about what money can buy. The data suggest the second.

The saving rate adds another layer. The personal saving rate was 4.1% in October 2026, down from 4.9% in October 2025. Real consumer spending, inflation-adjusted, was up 2.5% in the 12 months to October 2026. Households are still spending. Some of that spending is being financed by saving less. That is a fragile way to keep the economy moving. It can work for a while, especially when wages are still rising and unemployment is low. It is not a steady state.

The Federal Reserve's policy rate is part of the same story. The federal funds rate was 3.63% from May to August 2026 and has risen since to 3.88% in October 2026. The central bank is not trying to push borrowing costs back to the early 2020s. It is trying to keep inflation from becoming entrenched. The mortgage rate, however, is not simply the policy rate plus a fixed spread. It also contains expectations about future policy, the risk of long-term inflation, and the cost of funding a 30-year loan. When those expectations are unsettled, the 30-year rate stays high even if the policy rate is only moderately above its old level.

This is where the argument gets more complicated. The evidence does not show which households are hit hardest. There is no income-group or renter/owner breakdown here. It does not prove that mortgage rates cause the sentiment slump. And it cannot say whether the 2026 rise in headline inflation will persist. What it does show is a pattern: the price level is high, borrowing costs are high, saving is falling, and sentiment is weak.

The likeliest explanation is that households are not just reacting to the latest inflation number. They are reacting to a change in the cost of the things they use to build a stable life: a home, a car, a business, a degree, a buffer for emergencies. Inflation makes those things more expensive. High borrowing costs make them harder to finance. When both are true at once, the future looks less generous.

That is why the "inflation is falling" story was always a story about the rate, while households live with the level. A lower rate can make the news feel better without making the mortgage, the car loan or the student loan feel cheaper. A rising rate can make the news feel worse, but the deeper problem is the accumulated price level and the cost of credit that has not returned to the early 2020s.

The path from here depends on two things. First, whether food and energy inflation cools enough for headline inflation to stop pulling sentiment down. Second, whether long-term borrowing costs ease enough for households to feel the housing and credit ladder is moving again. The first is a macroeconomic question. The second is a household question. The first may be answered by the next CPI report. The second will be answered by whether a family can imagine buying, borrowing or moving without feeling that the cost of the future has gone up.

For now, the numbers point to a household economy that is still spending, but with less slack. The saving rate is lower. Sentiment is weak. Prices are up 29.8% since January 2020. Pay is up 33.2%, but the mortgage rate is 7.33%, not 2.74%. That is the tension: the average worker has not been wiped out by inflation, but the cost of getting ahead has not fallen with it. The question is whether households can keep borrowing less, saving less and feeling worse while still spending enough to keep the economy moving.

Headline inflation was 3.7%. The gap matters: food and energy are doing a lot of the work in the current print. That is also why the emotional response is strong. People buy groceries weekly and fill the tank regularly. They do not buy a house every year, but they feel every month that the house is farther away.

The labour market is not the obvious explanation. Unemployment was 4.1% in October 2026. That is not a jobs crisis. Average hourly earnings grew 3.2% in the year to September 2026, which is slower than prices, so the average worker's pay is again losing purchasing power. But the sentiment data are far weaker than the jobs data. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index was 48 in October 2026, against 71.7 in January 2025 and a low of 44.8 in May 2026. The University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers put it plainly: "Since mid-2022, consumer sentiment has been moving more closely with sentiment regarding higher prices than with sentiment regarding income."

That quote is useful because it separates income and prices. If people feel poorer because they are earning less, the policy conversation is about jobs. If they feel poorer because prices have risen, the conversation is about what money can buy. The data suggest the second.

The saving rate adds another layer. The personal saving rate was 4.1% in October 2026, down from 4.9% in October 2025. Real consumer spending, inflation-adjusted, was up 2.5% in the 12 months to October 2026. Households are still spending. Some of that spending is being financed by saving less.
