To understand how bond yields affect the wider economy, it is helpful to first think of a government bond as a loan made by an investor to the government. The yield is essentially the interest rate the government pays the investor for that loan. Because government bonds are considered some of the safest investments in the world, their yield acts as a benchmark or a starting point for almost every other type of interest rate in the financial system.

When bond yields rise, mortgage rates typically follow. Banks and mortgage lenders do not just set rates based on the central bank's base rate; they also look at the yields on long-term government bonds to estimate what it will cost them to borrow money over the next few years. If the government is paying more to borrow, lenders will raise the rates they charge homeowners to maintain their own profit margins. This means that as yields climb, new mortgages and existing tracker mortgages become more expensive, leaving households with less disposable income to spend elsewhere.

Company borrowing is affected in a similar way. Many large corporations raise money by issuing their own corporate bonds. Investors will always demand a higher return for holding corporate debt than they would for government debt, because companies are more likely to go bust than a national government. Therefore, if the baseline government yield rises, companies must offer even higher yields on their own bonds to attract buyers. This increases the cost of debt for businesses, which can eat into their profits or discourage them from investing in new projects, factories, or staff.

Finally, rising bond yields often put downward pressure on share prices. This happens for two main reasons. First, as mentioned, higher borrowing costs can reduce a company's future earnings, making the business less valuable. Second, investors constantly compare the potential returns of the stock market against the guaranteed returns of bonds. When bond yields are very low, investors are more likely to take risks by buying shares to get a decent return. However, when bond yields rise, bonds become a more attractive, safer alternative. Some investors will sell their shares to buy bonds instead, which drives share prices down. In short, when the cost of "safe" money goes up, the cost of borrowing rises for everyone, and the relative appeal of risky investments like stocks tends to fall.
