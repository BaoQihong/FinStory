To understand how rising bond yields ripple through the economy, it is helpful to first understand what a bond yield represents. A bond is essentially a loan made by an investor to a borrower, such as a government or a corporation. The yield is the annual return an investor earns on that bond. When bond yields rise, it means new bonds are being issued with higher interest payments to attract buyers. Because government bonds are considered very safe, their yields act as a benchmark or a baseline for almost all other types of borrowing.

Mortgage rates are closely tied to these benchmarks. Banks and lenders typically price mortgages by taking a baseline yield—often derived from government bond rates—and adding a margin to cover their risk and profit. When bond yields climb, the baseline rises, and lenders raise mortgage rates to remain competitive and maintain their profit margins. For homeowners and prospective buyers, this means higher monthly payments, reduced affordability, and a general cooling of the housing market.

Company borrowing is affected in a similar fashion. Corporations issue their own bonds to raise capital for expansion or operations. If investors can get a higher yield from safe government bonds, they will demand even higher yields from corporate bonds to compensate for the added risk of lending to a private company. Consequently, when benchmark yields rise, the cost for companies to issue new debt increases. Existing loans with floating interest rates also become more expensive to service. Higher borrowing costs eat into corporate profits and may lead companies to scale back investment plans or slow their growth.

Finally, rising bond yields exert downward pressure on share prices through two main channels: valuation and competition. First, analysts value stocks by forecasting future earnings and discounting them back to present value using a discount rate tied to bond yields. When yields rise, the discount rate increases, which mathematically lowers the present value of those future earnings, making stocks appear less valuable. Second, bonds and stocks compete for investor capital. When bonds offer higher, guaranteed yields, they become more attractive relative to the riskier equity market. Investors may sell stocks to lock in these higher bond returns, driving share prices down.

In summary, bond yields function like a gravitational force on financial markets. When yields rise, the cost of borrowing increases for both households and businesses, corporate profitability is squeezed, and the relative appeal of stocks diminishes. This interconnectedness explains why a spike in the bond market often precedes volatility in housing and stock markets.
