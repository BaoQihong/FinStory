To understand how bond yields influence the broader economy, it is helpful to first view a government bond as a loan made by an investor to the government. The yield is essentially the return, or interest rate, the investor earns on that loan. Because government bonds are considered some of the safest investments in the world, they act as a benchmark for almost every other type of debt. When bond yields rise, it creates a ripple effect that pushes other interest rates upward.

Mortgage rates are closely tied to these yields. Banks and lenders do not lend money for free; they want to earn a profit that is competitive with other safe investments. If a lender can get a higher return by simply buying a government bond, they will raise the interest rates they charge on mortgages to ensure they are still making a worthwhile profit. Consequently, as bond yields climb, the cost of borrowing for a home increases. This makes monthly payments more expensive for new buyers and those refinancing, which typically cools the housing market by reducing demand.

Company borrowing follows a similar logic. Most large corporations raise money by issuing corporate bonds. Investors demand a higher yield on corporate bonds than on government bonds because companies carry a higher risk of going bankrupt. Therefore, if the baseline government yield rises, the interest rate a company must pay to attract investors also rises. This increases the cost of doing business. When it becomes more expensive to borrow money to build new factories or develop new products, companies may scale back their growth plans or spend more of their earnings on interest payments rather than on innovation.

Finally, rising bond yields often put downward pressure on share prices. This happens for two main reasons. First, as mentioned, higher borrowing costs eat into corporate profits, which makes a company less valuable. Second, investors constantly compare the potential returns of stocks against the safety of bonds. Stocks are risky; bonds are stable. When bond yields are very low, investors are willing to take risks on stocks to get a decent return. However, when bond yields rise, the "safe" option becomes more attractive. Some investors will sell their stocks to lock in the higher, guaranteed returns of bonds. This shift in demand leads to a drop in stock prices.

In short, bond yields act like a gravitational pull on the financial world. When they rise, the cost of debt goes up for homeowners and businesses, and the relative appeal of the stock market declines, often slowing down overall economic activity.
