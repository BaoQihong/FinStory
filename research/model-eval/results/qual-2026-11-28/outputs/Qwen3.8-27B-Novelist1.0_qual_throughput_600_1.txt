When you hear that bond yields have risen, it means the interest rate that lenders effectively demand for lending money has gone up. This happens because bond prices and yields move in opposite directions: when investors sell bonds, prices fall and yields rise, and vice versa. The most important benchmark is usually the ten-year government bond, because it is seen as relatively risk-free and sets a baseline for everything else.

Mortgage rates are closely tied to this baseline. Banks and other lenders fund their mortgage books partly by borrowing in the bond market, so when their own borrowing costs rise, they pass some of that on to homebuyers. A rise of, say, half a percentage point in long-term government yields will typically show up within weeks as higher fixed-rate mortgage deals. This makes monthly payments more expensive, cools demand for houses, and can put downward pressure on house prices over time.

Company borrowing is affected in a similar but more direct way. Firms that issue bonds to raise capital must now offer higher coupon rates to attract investors, which raises their cost of debt. Companies that rely on bank loans also face higher rates, because banks' wholesale funding costs have risen. The result is that fewer marginal projects look profitable, so business investment slows. Companies with lots of existing floating-rate debt see their interest bills jump almost immediately, squeezing profits.

Share prices tend to fall when yields rise, for two main reasons. First, higher borrowing costs reduce expected future profits for companies, especially leveraged ones. Second, and perhaps more subtly, bond yields are used as a discount rate when valuing stocks: the further into the future a company's cash flows fall, the more their present value is reduced by a higher discount rate. This hits long-duration assets hardest, which is why growth and technology stocks often fall more sharply than dividend-paying value stocks when yields spike. There is also a simple substitution effect: when bonds start offering a respectable risk-free return, some investors move money out of riskier equities and into the safety of government debt.

It is worth noting that the relationship is not perfectly mechanical. If rising yields reflect a strong, healthy economy, stocks may still do well because corporate earnings grow faster than the discount rate rises. Conversely, if yields rise because of inflation fears or a loss of confidence in government finances, the negative effect on equities tends to dominate. But as a rule of thumb, a rise in bond yields makes borrowing dearer for households and firms, and makes shares look less attractive relative to bonds.
