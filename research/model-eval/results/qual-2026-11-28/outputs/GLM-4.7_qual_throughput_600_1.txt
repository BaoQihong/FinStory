When the yield on government bonds climbs, it acts like a domino falling over, knocking into the wider economy and influencing everything from the monthly cost of a home loan to the value of your stock portfolio. To understand this, it helps to view bond yields as the baseline interest rate for the entire financial system. When that baseline moves up, almost every other cost of borrowing moves with it.

The most immediate impact is usually felt in the housing market. Mortgages are directly priced off government bonds, specifically long-term ones. When investors demand a higher return to lend money to the government, lenders charge homebuyers a higher rate to match that risk. For a homeowner, this means that as bond yields rise, the interest rate on a new fixed-rate mortgage will increase. This results in higher monthly payments, which cools down demand for homes because fewer people can afford the steep borrowing costs.

The effect spreads to the business world as well. When government bonds pay more, they become very attractive investments compared to the riskier proposition of lending money to a corporation. To entice investors to buy their debt instead of safe government bonds, companies must offer higher interest rates on the bonds they issue. This makes borrowing money significantly more expensive for businesses. Consequently, a company looking to build a new factory or buy new equipment might have to pay much more in interest payments, which can eat into their profits or cause them to cancel those expansion plans entirely.

Finally, rising yields tend to drag down share prices. There are two main reasons for this. First, higher borrowing costs hurt a company’s bottom line, making the business less valuable because it has less money left over after paying interest. Second, and perhaps more importantly, rising yields change the math of how stocks are valued. Stocks are bought for their future potential earnings, but those future cash flows are worth less in today’s money when interest rates are high. When a safe government bond offers a high, guaranteed return, the riskier potential growth of a stock becomes less appealing. Investors often sell stocks to buy bonds, causing share prices to drop. In short, a rise in yields makes money more expensive to borrow and reduces the value of future profits, creating a challenging environment for both borrowers and investors.
