To understand how bond yields ripple through the broader economy, it helps to first grasp what a bond yield actually represents. At its core, a bond yield is the effective interest rate an investor earns on a loan they have made to a government or corporation. When investors become nervous about the economy or expect inflation to rise, they demand higher returns to compensate for the perceived risk of lending their money. This drives bond prices down and, conversely, pushes bond yields up. This shift in the "risk-free" rate, typically anchored by government bonds, acts as a baseline for all other interest rates in the economy, creating a domino effect that touches everything from home buying to stock market valuations.

The most immediate impact of rising bond yields is felt in the housing market through mortgage rates. Mortgage lenders do not set their rates in a vacuum; they look closely at long-term government bond yields, particularly the ten-year Treasury note in the United States. Because mortgages are long-term loans, lenders need to know what they could earn if they simply lent money to the government instead. If bond yields rise, lenders must offer higher mortgage rates to remain competitive and profitable. Consequently, when bond yields climb, mortgage rates almost always follow suit. This makes borrowing money to buy a home significantly more expensive. For potential homebuyers, this means higher monthly payments, which reduces purchasing power and can cool down housing demand, potentially leading to slower price growth or even declines in home values.

Beyond the housing market, rising yields directly increase the cost of capital for businesses. Companies often rely on issuing corporate bonds to fund expansion, research, or daily operations. When the baseline interest rate rises due to higher government bond yields, investors will demand higher interest payments from corporations as well. This makes it more expensive for companies to borrow money. For highly profitable companies with strong cash flows, this might be a manageable inconvenience. However, for smaller firms or those with high existing debt loads, higher borrowing costs can squeeze profit margins. They may have to cut back on investment, delay new projects, or reduce hiring to manage their increased interest expenses. This tightening of financial conditions can slow down overall economic growth.

Finally, rising bond yields exert downward pressure on share prices, particularly for growth-oriented stocks. Stock valuations are largely based on the present value of a company’s future earnings. When interest rates are low, future profits are discounted at a lower rate, making them look more valuable today. However, when bond yields rise, the discount rate increases, which mathematically reduces the present value of those future earnings. Furthermore, higher yields make bonds more attractive relative to stocks. If investors can get a safe, guaranteed return of five percent from government bonds, they are less willing to take the risk of buying stocks unless those stocks offer significantly higher potential returns. This shift in investor preference often leads to a sell-off in equities, causing share prices to fall as money moves from the risky stock market to the safer bond market.
