When bond yields rise, it means investors can earn more interest from lending money to governments or other bond issuers. Because bonds are now more attractive, their prices fall, and the higher yield becomes a new benchmark for the cost of money across the economy. Mortgage lenders do not simply copy government bond yields, but they do fund themselves in wholesale markets where those yields matter. If the yield on government bonds and the related swap rates that lenders use rise, the cost of funding fixed-rate mortgages tends to go up. Lenders usually respond by raising rates on new fixed mortgages, and sometimes on variable ones too if market expectations for central-bank rates have shifted. Existing fixed-rate borrowers are unaffected until their deal ends, but anyone buying a home, remortgaging or taking out a new loan will probably face higher monthly payments. Higher mortgage rates also reduce how much buyers can borrow, which can cool demand in the housing market.

For companies, a rise in bond yields works in a similar way but with an extra layer of risk. Companies issue bonds to borrow money for expansion, refinancing or day-to-day operations. When the general level of bond yields rises, investors demand more interest to lend to companies as well, especially weaker or riskier ones. That makes new borrowing more expensive and can make banks more cautious about lending. Firms may delay projects, cut investment or choose to borrow less. Companies with large existing debts may see interest costs climb when loans or bonds mature and need refinancing, leaving less money for wages, dividends or investment. Some firms are more exposed than others: young, fast-growing companies and heavily indebted businesses feel the pinch more than cash-rich firms with little debt.

Share prices are affected because higher bond yields change how investors value future profits. A share is a claim on a company’s future earnings, and those earnings are worth less today when investors can get a higher safe return from bonds. Higher yields also raise the interest rate used to discount future cash flows, which tends to push valuations down, particularly for growth companies whose profits are expected far in the future. At the same time, bonds become a stronger competitor to shares: why take equity risk if government bonds pay more? That can pull money out of the stock market or make investors demand a higher return for holding shares. The overall effect is not automatic or uniform. Share prices may still rise if company profits are strong, and some sectors such as banks can benefit from higher rates. But broadly, a sharp rise in bond yields tends to make mortgages and company borrowing more expensive and to put pressure on share prices, especially for borrowers and growth-focused companies.
