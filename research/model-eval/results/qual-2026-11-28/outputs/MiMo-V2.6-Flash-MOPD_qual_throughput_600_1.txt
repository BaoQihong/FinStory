When bond yields rise, it is like the price of borrowing money for the government has gone up. Because government bonds are considered the safest place to lend money, their yields set a baseline for all other interest rates in the economy. When the government has to pay more to attract lenders, everyone else has to pay more too.

This directly impacts mortgage rates. Banks and lenders use the yield on long-term government bonds as a starting point when they calculate how much interest to charge on a home loan. They add a margin to that yield to cover their own costs and profits. So, if the government bond yield goes from 4% to 5%, a mortgage lender will likely increase their rates by a similar amount to stay profitable. For a homebuyer, this means their monthly payments will be higher, and they can afford a smaller loan for the same monthly budget. For existing homeowners with variable-rate mortgages, their payments will also increase.

The same logic applies to companies. When a business needs to borrow money to expand, buy new equipment, or cover its operations, it looks at the bond market. The interest rate a company must pay on its own bonds is directly linked to the government bond yield. A higher baseline means the company’s borrowing costs will rise. This makes it more expensive for them to fund growth, which can slow down the economy. It also means they have less money left over for other things, like hiring new staff or investing in research.

Finally, rising bond yields put pressure on share prices. Investors are always looking for the best return on their money. When bond yields are low, the stock market looks more attractive because it offers the potential for higher growth. However, as bond yields rise, the "safe" option of government bonds becomes more appealing. Investors can now get a decent, guaranteed return without the risk of the stock market. This causes some investors to sell their shares and move their money into bonds. This selling pressure, combined with the fact that higher borrowing costs will likely reduce company profits, pushes share prices down. In short, rising yields make bonds a more competitive investment, drawing money away from stocks and making it more expensive for companies to operate.
