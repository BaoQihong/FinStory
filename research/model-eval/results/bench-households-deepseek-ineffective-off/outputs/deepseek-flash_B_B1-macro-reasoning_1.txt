## The story is not “strong consumers versus gloomy consumers”

The evidence points to a more specific feature: **disinflation without much relief for rate-sensitive households, and aggregate resilience that may be masking distributional strain.** The table shows CPI inflation lower, core inflation lower but still near 3%, the fed funds rate down about a percentage point, yet the 30-year mortgage rate essentially unchanged and the 10-year yield slightly higher. Unemployment is low, real wages are positive, and real spending is still growing—but more slowly—while the saving rate has fallen and sentiment has dropped sharply. That combination does not prove consumers are fine, nor that they are collapsing. It invites competing explanations.

## Competing explanations, and how to distinguish them

**1. Income-supported resilience.** Real wages are positive on this table: average hourly earnings growth exceeds CPI inflation. If spending is being carried by real income and employment, then spending should be broad across income groups, saving should be stable or rising, and credit stress should not be worsening. If instead spending is concentrated among high-income or asset-owning households and financed by lower saving or credit, the “resilient consumer” label is misleading.

**2. Fixed-rate lock-in and wealth effects.** Elevated policy rates may be failing to reach existing homeowners with low fixed mortgages; higher rates also raise interest income for savers and can support asset values for owners. This predicts weak housing turnover and new mortgage activity, strong spending by older/wealthier owners, and acute pressure on renters and first-time buyers. It also explains why Fed easing has not lowered the mortgage rate much: long-term yields and mortgage spreads matter more than the policy rate.

**3. Delayed restriction.** The Fed’s tightening may still be working through lags. If so, interest-sensitive sectors—housing, durables, small-business credit—should be weak, delinquencies should be rising, and spending growth should continue to decelerate. The fall in real spending growth from about 3.0% to 2.5% is consistent with cooling, but not proof of a break.

**4. Supply-side disinflation.** Inflation may be falling because supply chains, labor supply, and shelter inflation are normalizing, not because demand is weak. That would show up in goods versus services components and in inflation expectations. It would also mean low unemployment and positive spending are not contradictory.

**5. Sentiment as price-level and political noise.** The sentiment drop may reflect the cumulative price level, housing affordability, and partisan/media effects rather than current unemployment. If so, sentiment should diverge from spending and be poorly explained by local labor-market conditions. Microdata on sentiment by income and party affiliation would test this.

## Transmission channels

The key channel is **household debt repricing**. Existing fixed-rate mortgage holders are largely insulated; new buyers, renters, and households with credit-card, auto, or personal-loan debt are not. The mortgage rate’s failure to fall alongside the fed funds rate is central: it limits relief for the marginal buyer and keeps affordability tight. Higher rates also reach households through tighter credit standards, weaker hiring, and lower asset prices—though the table does not show asset prices or credit conditions.

There is also a **saving-buffer channel**. The personal saving rate has fallen, which can support spending temporarily but leaves less cushion. That is not necessarily alarming at 5.5%, but it means aggregate spending may be less durable than it looks. A **wealth channel** cuts the other way for owners: higher rates can depress bond and housing valuations, but wealthy households may still benefit from interest income and accumulated equity.

## Who is affected differently

The likely split is not “consumers” versus “consumers.” It is **fixed-rate owners versus renters and new borrowers; savers versus debtors; high-income versus low-income households; older versus younger households; large firms versus small businesses.** A homeowner with a low fixed mortgage and cash savings may feel little restriction. A renter facing high rents, a first-time buyer facing a 6%-plus mortgage, or a low-income household carrying credit-card debt faces a very different rate environment. The aggregate spending number cannot tell us which group is driving it.

## What the conventional reading misses

The conventional reading— “inflation is falling, unemployment is low, spending is holding up, so the consumer is fine”—misses four things. First, **inflation is a rate; the price level is what households experience**. Lower inflation does not undo past increases.
